  ones). Strategy tables and value-focused brainstorming are advocated without controlled evidence in the

# Decision-analysis and JDM research on generating alternatives and objectives

**Facet question.** What does decision analysis (value-focused thinking, strategy tables) and
judgment-and-decision-making (JDM) research measure about the incompleteness of unaided generation of
alternatives and objectives, which prompts or structures change it, at what cost to quality, and how do
generators judge their own completeness?

**Evidence-level vocabulary used below.** *Advocated* = a method is prescribed or illustrated by an
application without a comparison. *Demonstrated* = an application or observational study reports
outcomes without a controlled comparison. *Measured* = a controlled or quasi-experimental design reports
quantities with a comparison condition. Abstract-only sources carry `{confidence: abstract-only}`;
figures that come from a university press office rather than the paper carry a "release reports" framing.

## 1. Methods and how each generates or checks alternatives

### 1.1 Value-focused thinking (objectives first) versus alternative-focused thinking

**How it generates.** Objectives are articulated before alternatives, and the objectives are then used as
stimuli to create alternatives. Siebert and Keeney study "the effectiveness of different stimuli" for
alternative creation across five studies of personally relevant decisions
[siebert-keeney-alternatives-2015]{450} {confidence: abstract-only}. US structured-decision-making
training states the rationale as a "natural human tendency to focus first on alternatives ... rather than
focusing first on objectives," with the risks that "We tend to limit ourselves to a smaller set of
alternatives" and "may anchor on the first suggested alternative"
[usfws-usgs-sdm-developing-alternatives-2011]{458}.

**Evidence level: measured (incompleteness and stimulus effect), with access limits.**
- Incompleteness: for decisions where the full set is "not readily apparent," decision makers "identify
  less than half of their alternatives and ... the average quality of the overlooked alternatives is the
  same as those identified" [siebert-keeney-alternatives-2015]{450} {confidence: abstract-only}. The
  Bayreuth release reports the internship study (about 200 business students): unaided, students
  identified 37% of the alternatives they later rated relevant on a comprehensive master list; only 44% had
  considered the option they later rated best and 10% their top three in advance
  [siebert-keeney-press-2015]{451}.
- Stimulus effect: four studies "confirm with high significance that such use [of objectives] enhances
  both the number and quality of created alternatives" [siebert-keeney-alternatives-2015]{450}
  {confidence: abstract-only}. The release reports that, among about 400 students, those who first
  articulated objectives found twice as many alternatives, the objective-informed set was rated higher in
  58% of cases, a master list of objectives with instructions to use it raised alternatives considered by
  53%, and "Mehr Zeit ist kein Ersatz" (more time is no substitute) [siebert-keeney-press-2015]{451}.
- Experience moderation: a 2016 study (236 students and researchers; generating alternative titles for a
  paper) is reported as showing that reflection on objectives raised alternative quality regardless of
  prior experience, that experienced participants generally produced better alternatives, and that more
  objectives considered went with better alternatives [siebert-novices-press-2016]{454}.
- Group workshops: eight value-focused versus six alternative-focused stakeholder workshops (7–10 people
  each) on salmon-habitat risk; the abstract reports that the value-focused structure "led to more
  thoughtful and better informed risk management decisions"
  [arvai-gregory-mcdaniels-vft-risk-communication-2001]{456} {confidence: abstract-only}. The outcome is
  decision quality and deliberation, not a count of alternatives.
- Organizational field data: across 356 organizational decisions, the "objectives" direction-setting tactic
  (30% of cases) had 70% sustained and 58% full use, versus 56% and 42% for the "idea" tactic (37% of
  cases), which "prompts managers to focus on a single solution"
  [nutt-half-decisions-fail-1999]{465}. These are interview-based case outcomes measured as adoption,
  without random assignment.

### 1.2 The generate-then-recognize master-list paradigm (a completeness check)

**How it checks.** The generator lists items unaided, then marks relevant items on a "master list,"
then maps own items onto it; checked-but-unmapped items are counted as omissions ("recognized") and
rated for importance [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}. Master lists were built by experimenters
"through brainstorming, internal discussion, and communication with prior MBA students," augmented with
popular write-ins from earlier participants, or compiled from participants' own responses plus the
authors' analyses [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}. The same design underlies the 37% figure
in 1.1 [siebert-keeney-press-2015]{451}.

**Evidence level: measured.** Bond, Carlson and Keeney (objectives, not alternatives):

| Study (decision) | n | Self-generated | Recognized (missed) | Importance: missed vs self |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 MBA program (retrospective) | 62 | 6.0 | 7.6 | 10% missed their top objective; 71% missed ≥1 of top five |
| S2 executive MBA (retrospective, 1-week delay before ranking) | 70 | 6.6 | 7.7 | mean rank 5.8 vs 5.1; 35% missed top objective |
| S3 internship (prospective, 1 week of rumination) | 28 | 6.8 | 7.9 | 6.8 vs 7.4 on 1–9 (bogus item 2.9) |
| Seagate case (12 executives, value-focused probing interviews) | 12 | 36% of 39 aggregate objectives per person | — | aggregate endorsed by the group |

All rows from [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}. Across the three lab studies, participants
"consistently omitted nearly half of the objectives that they later identified as personally relevant,"
and omitted objectives were "almost as important" as generated ones
[bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}. A later reanalysis placed first-attempt yield at "30% to 50%"
[bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}.

**Failure modes of the check itself.**
- The paradigm's denominator is the master list, which is itself generated: a template of about 30
  objectives "even when viewed after successful interventions, allowed individuals to identify many
  additional relevant objectives" [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}, and lists were
  expanded between studies with write-ins participants supplied
  [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}. The recall figure is relative to a reference that is not
  shown to be complete. {inferred: aggregate}
- Self-mapping was liberal (e.g. "multicultural ... coworkers" mapped to "interact with senior
  managers"); the authors judge "the mapping procedure overstates the completeness of participants'
  self-generated lists" [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}.
- The authors state the research "offers no direct evidence that the inadequacies ... would degrade the
  quality of eventual decisions" [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}.

### 1.3 Diagnosis: too narrow versus too shallow

Bond, Carlson and Keeney split the failure into "not thinking broadly enough about the range of relevant
objectives, and not thinking deeply enough to articulate every objective within the range that is
considered" [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}. Reanalysing 2008 data with the master
list partitioned into four mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive conceptual categories,
self-generation concentrated in each person's top category (e.g. 2.60 observed vs 1.84 under a random
model; 0.58 vs 1.36 in the lowest category) — "breadth of thinking was insufficient"
[bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}. In a pretest, ~49% of respondents attributed misses
to too-narrow and ~36% to too-shallow thinking, and 92% acknowledged their list was incomplete once
confronted with the 36-item list [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}.

The 2008 paper also tested an effort account and rejected it: the correlation between numbers
self-generated and recognized was slightly positive (r = 0.18, ns), not negative, and the authors
attribute misses to "an incomplete cognitive representation of the decision"
[bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}.

### 1.4 Prompts and stimuli with measured effects (Bond, Carlson & Keeney 2010)

| Intervention (design) | Effect on quantity | Effect on misses / quality |
|---|---|---|
| Second pass structured by five categories after an unaided list (S1, n=20, within-subject) | +4.5 listed; uniquely mapped additions raised self-generated set by over 30% | added items 7.3 vs 7.7 importance (ns); category vs second-chance effect confounded |
| Upfront categories vs standard vs four examples (S2, n=158, between-subject) | categories 12.7 vs standard 9.2 listed (F(1,155)=16.00); examples 10.45 adjusted (ns vs standard) | missed: standard 7.9, categories 7.9, examples 10.8 — examples missed reliably more |
| Second pass "simple review" (S3, n=295, six arms) | +0.55 (20% added any) | baseline |
| "Deeper review" — warning that people list <50% and missed items matter as much | +2.77 (87% added any) | added vs initial importance 7.23 vs 7.28 (ns overall) |
| Warning + "add at least three / six / nine" | +3.08 / +3.90 / +4.87; target regression β = 0.30 | no quality loss in successful arms |
| Warning + four categories | +3.56 | not reliably better than deeper review (p = 0.134) |

All rows from [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}. The authors' reading: sample
objectives may "hinder generation by anchoring respondents on the specific examples provided" (consistent
with part-list cueing); upfront categories yield "more specific (but not more complete) sets"; "simply
asking decision makers to add objectives was virtually ineffective," whereas telling them research shows
most people can add more produced substantial gains; and "for individuals already motivated to think more
deeply, the benefit of categories was minimal" [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}.
Their prescription is to generate independently first, consult any template only afterwards, and aim to
"approximately double" the initial count [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}.

Interventions that did **not** close the gap: one week of rumination with a mid-week reminder and
encouragement to consult others (S3 of 2008: still 6.8 generated vs 7.9 missed), with the conclusion
that "merely considering the decision at multiple points in time is insufficient"
[bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}; and, as reported for alternatives, more time
[siebert-keeney-press-2015]{451}.

### 1.5 Aggregation of independent generators

In the Seagate case, each of 12 executives listed on average 36% of the 39 specific objectives; "the
aggregated list ... was a much more complete set" and was endorsed by the group
[bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}. The 2010 prescription for groups is that each member
generates independently and tries to double their list before discussion, because "communication may
redirect individuals' from their own ideas to those that they hear expressed"; the group set is the
de-duplicated union [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}. Value-focused brainstorming
builds the same sequencing in — "all participants ... individually create alternatives prior to any
anchoring on group discussions" — plus objectives as explicit guides
[keeney-value-focused-brainstorming-2012]{455} {confidence: abstract-only}. Evidence level: demonstrated
(one case study) for the union effect; advocated for value-focused brainstorming, whose abstract
describes an application (building-evacuation alternatives after the World Trade Center disaster) rather
than a comparison [keeney-value-focused-brainstorming-2012]{455} {confidence: abstract-only}.

### 1.6 Strategy (generation) tables

**How it generates.** "The strategy-generation table helps in finding creative alternatives"
[howard-decision-analysis-practice-promise-1988]{457} {confidence: abstract-only}. SDM training
describes the mechanics: list options under each decision element (columns such as Habitat Protection /
Predator Control / Enhance Population / Monitor), then "Select a coherent set among those to be one
Strategy," naming each strategy by a theme ("Maintain Existing Population", "On the Go (Dispersal)",
"Increase Population to Carrying Capacity"); a second route builds strategies "Based on alternative
hypotheses about limiting factors" [usfws-usgs-sdm-developing-alternatives-2011]{458}. The same module
lists companion moves — address conflicting objectives by imagining the best consequence for each
objective and building an alternative for it, then hybrids; challenge real and perceived constraints;
"Develop alternatives before assessing feasibility"; portfolios of like elements bounded by constraints;
and "Distinctiveness – Outcomes of alternatives should be discrete from each other"
[usfws-usgs-sdm-developing-alternatives-2011]{458}.

**Evidence level: advocated.** Neither attested source reports a controlled test of strategy tables'
effect on the number, distinctness or quality of alternatives
[howard-decision-analysis-practice-promise-1988]{457} [usfws-usgs-sdm-developing-alternatives-2011]{458}.

### 1.7 Option generation in JDM: fluency, diversity and quality

**How diversity is measured.** Del Missier, Visentini and Mäntylä score diversity as the number of
predefined response categories accessed — "akin to the 'limb' of the option trees in Gettys et al.,
1987" — with categories built from prior studies and domain literature and refined after a pilot (kappa >
0.85) [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}. People accessing more categories also generated
more options (r = 0.52, 0.45, 0.66) [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}.

**Measured findings (N = 141; three ill-structured problems).** Ideation fluency (alternative uses)
added ΔR² of 0.14–0.19 to option-generation fluency beyond memory, executive control, intelligence and
domain knowledge [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}. Fluency correlated negatively with
mean option quality (controlling for the best option) in two problems (−0.18, −0.21) and positively with
the best option's quality in two (0.27, 0.31); fluency–choice-quality correlations were all
non-significant and indirect effects "of small magnitude"
[delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}. In everyday-scenario generation (N = 48), number of
options correlated with set divergence (r = 0.62) and maximum originality (0.53) but not mean
originality (0.11), and negatively with mean feasibility (−0.40)
[schweizer-creative-option-generation-2016]{460}.

**Earlier act-generation work, cited through Del Missier et al.** They attribute to Gettys et al. (1987)
and Manning et al. (1980) marked departures from optimal generation (campus parking, housing for an
impecunious friend, uses for a geographic area), to Mehle (1982) and Klein et al. (1995) suboptimal
generation in expert mechanics and experienced chess players, and report that these were "replicated
even when providing very ample generation time or incentives for the quality or quantity of generated
options (Gettys et al., 1987)" [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}. They also attribute to
Mehle (1982) and Gettys et al. (1987) that participants "think they have generated a large fraction of
the potential options, even if they have identified only a limited part"
[delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}. These are cite-through claims; the primary papers were
not read.

**Set composition.** The 2016 Bayreuth release reports that "a set of few but clearly distinguishable
alternatives scored better than a set of many but mutually similar alternatives" when whole sets were
rated [siebert-novices-press-2016]{454}.

### 1.8 Naturalistic decision making: one option, recognized

The recognition-primed decision model holds that experienced fireground commanders "identify and carry
out a course of action without having to generate and analyze a set of options," and that "people can
use experience to generate a plausible option as the first one they consider"
[klein-rpd-model-overview]{463}. In chess, a study of players above Elo 2000 is reported as finding about
three options generated (M = 3.11), the first often chosen, and quality declining from first to third;
a conceptual replication with players below Elo 2000 (N = 92) found M = 2.2 options, first chosen as best
in 37.3% of cases, and no significant serial-position effect on quality (F(3, 992) = 1.73, p = .159),
concluding that Take-the-First "becomes reliably effective only at higher levels of expertise"
[raab-chess-take-the-first-replication-2026]{462}. Del Missier et al. attribute to Johnson and Raab
(2003), with handball players under time pressure, that average option-set quality falls as more options
are generated and that generating more led to poorer, less consistent choices, "but see Ward et al.,
2011, for contrasting findings" [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}.

### 1.9 Illusory completeness of a decomposition

Fischhoff, Slovic and Lichtenstein varied fault trees for "a car fails to start." When major branches
were pruned, the "all other problems" branch should have risen from .078 to .468 but "was only doubled";
directing attention to "other" raised it by about 50% (e.g. .140 to .217), "still much less than it
should have been," without overcorrection [fischhoff-slovic-lichtenstein-fault-trees-1978]{464}.
Experienced mechanics assigned .215 where .441 was implied, and self-rated knowledge did not track
sensitivity; "Exaggeration of fault tree completeness was found with both 'regular' subjects and
experienced mechanics" [fischhoff-slovic-lichtenstein-fault-trees-1978]{464}. Splitting a branch into
two increased its perceived importance [fischhoff-slovic-lichtenstein-fault-trees-1978]{464}. The
explanation favoured is availability — "what is out of sight is also out of mind"
[fischhoff-slovic-lichtenstein-fault-trees-1978]{464}.

### 1.10 Organizational search tactics

Across 356 decisions, imposition ("existing solution") was used in 223 cases with 55 sustained / 41 full
use, while integrated benchmarking (6%) had 71 / 78 [nutt-half-decisions-fail-1999]{465}. In 168 cases, a
"synthesized template" integrating practices from several sources "had the most success, but this
tactic was seldom used"; "cyclical search" succeeded under low importance and urgency; the design tactic
improved "when ... multiple alternatives were sought"; the imposed-idea tactic was "widely used and seldom
successful" [nutt-solution-ideas-1993]{466} {confidence: abstract-only}. Evidence level: demonstrated
(observational, adoption-based outcomes).

## 2. Evidence summary by method

| Method | What it does to the option space | Evidence level | Main limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Objectives as stimuli (VFT) | Seeds generation from each objective | Measured (S&K 2015; Siebert 2016), abstract/press access only | Students, personal decisions; full designs unaudited |

## Disconfirming analysis

Searched each load-bearing claim against the attested set before writing it.

- **"Prompts improve coverage."** Disconfirmed for specific prompts: upfront examples increased misses
  (10.8 vs 7.9), upfront categories did not reduce misses, a plain request to add more produced +0.55, and
  categories added nothing reliable over a warning [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453};
  more rumination did not close the gap [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}. Effective prompts in
  the measured set share a second pass plus an explicit claim that the first list is incomplete
  {inferred: convergence}.
- **"Objectives-first produces more and better alternatives."** No contrary result was found in the
  attested set, but the controlled evidence is held at abstract and press-release depth
  [siebert-keeney-alternatives-2015]{450} {confidence: abstract-only} [siebert-keeney-press-2015]{451}
  [siebert-novices-press-2016]{454}; the group-workshop test measures decision thoughtfulness, not
  coverage [arvai-gregory-mcdaniels-vft-risk-communication-2001]{456} {confidence: abstract-only}; the
  organizational advantage is observational [nutt-half-decisions-fail-1999]{465}.
- **"Incompleteness matters for decision quality."** The objectives authors disclaim direct evidence
  [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}. Fluency's relation to choice quality was non-significant
  directly and small indirectly [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}. The RPD and
  Take-the-First lines show competent decisions from one or a few options for experts in familiar,
  time-pressured tasks [klein-rpd-model-overview]{463} [raab-chess-take-the-first-replication-2026]{462}.
  Against this, overlooked alternatives were of equal average quality
  [siebert-keeney-alternatives-2015]{450} {confidence: abstract-only} and 44% had considered their
  eventually-best option [siebert-keeney-press-2015]{451}.
- **"The master-list figure measures true completeness."** Disconfirmed in part: the reference set is
  itself incomplete and grows across studies, and mapping inflates self-generated counts
  [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452} [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}. The
  two biases do not offset: liberal mapping and an incomplete reference list both push measured recall
  above true recall {inferred: aggregate}.
- **"Generators know when they are incomplete."** Disconfirmed: omission insensitivity in pruned fault
  trees for laypeople and mechanics [fischhoff-slovic-lichtenstein-fault-trees-1978]{464}; overconfidence
  about completeness attributed to Mehle and Gettys [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}.
  The 92% acknowledgment of incompleteness came only after exposure to a master list
  [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}.
- **"Strategy tables yield genuinely different alternatives."** No measured evidence for or against was
  found; only prescription [howard-decision-analysis-practice-promise-1988]{457} {confidence:
  abstract-only} [usfws-usgs-sdm-developing-alternatives-2011]{458}.
- **"Expertise protects against incompleteness."** Mixed: domain expertise mostly does not predict
  generation fluency (attributed to Mehle 1982; Klein et al. 1995)
  [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}; mechanics showed the same omission insensitivity
  [fischhoff-slovic-lichtenstein-fault-trees-1978]{464}; experienced participants produced better
  alternatives in the titles task [siebert-novices-press-2016]{454}; first-option quality advantage
  appeared only at high Elo [raab-chess-take-the-first-replication-2026]{462}.

## Contradictions

| Claim | Position A | Position B | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does generating more options help? | "Less is more": mean set quality falls and choices worsen with more options (Johnson & Raab, as reported) [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}; first option often best for experts [raab-chess-take-the-first-replication-2026]{462} | "Quantity breeds quality" for the best option: fluency raises max quality [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}; number of options tracks maximum originality [schweizer-creative-option-generation-2016]{460} | `qualifies` — the positions use different quality metrics (mean vs max) and populations (time-pressured experts vs untimed lay generators) |
| Value of more alternatives in a set | Few clearly distinguishable alternatives scored better than many similar ones [siebert-novices-press-2016]{454} | More objectives-stimulated alternatives raised number and quality [siebert-keeney-alternatives-2015]{450} {confidence: abstract-only} | `tension` — distinctness vs count; neither source, as attested, separates the two |
| Do categories help? | Second-pass categories raised the self-generated set by over 30% [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453} | Upfront categories did not reduce misses; categories added no reliable gain over a warning [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453} | `qualifies` — timing and motivation moderate the effect (same paper) |
| Expertise and generation | Expertise mostly not predictive of fluency [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459} | Experienced participants produce better alternatives [siebert-novices-press-2016]{454}; Take-the-First effective only at high expertise [raab-chess-take-the-first-replication-2026]{462} | `incommensurable` — fluency counts vs rated quality of alternatives vs first-option quality |
| Remedy strength for omissions | Focusing on "other" raised the residual estimate by about 50%, still well short of the implied value [fischhoff-slovic-lichtenstein-fault-trees-1978]{464} | A warning that first lists are incomplete raised second-pass additions from 0.55 to 2.77 [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453} | `incommensurable` — probability estimates for a given tree vs generation of new items |

## Transfer-relevant observations

Stated in the field's own terms; no project recommendations.

1. The field's completeness signal is **recall against an independently compiled reference list**
   (master list), reported as share recalled and as importance of missed versus generated items
   [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452} [siebert-keeney-press-2015]{451}. Its known biases are an
   incomplete reference list and lenient matching [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}
   [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}.
2. Unaided first passes recover roughly 30–50% of later-recognized items, and missed items are about as
   important as recovered ones, across objectives and alternatives
   [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453} [siebert-keeney-alternatives-2015]{450}
   {confidence: abstract-only} {inferred: convergence}.
3. Omissions concentrate in **categories the generator never entered** (narrowness), not only in depth
   within entered categories; a category partition of the reference list makes this visible
   [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}. Diversity is operationalized as count of
   predefined categories or option-tree limbs accessed [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459}.
4. Second passes work when the generator is told the first pass is typically incomplete and given a
   numeric target; plain "anything else?" requests, upfront examples, and extra time do not
   [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453} [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}
   [siebert-keeney-press-2015]{451}.
5. Generators and experts under-weight what a decomposition leaves out; a residual "other" bucket is
   systematically too small, and drawing attention to it closes only part of the gap
   [fischhoff-slovic-lichtenstein-fault-trees-1978]{464}.
6. Pooling independently produced lists before discussion yields a more complete set than any one
   contributor [bond-carlson-keeney-objectives-2008]{452}
   [bond-carlson-keeney-improving-objectives-2010]{453}.
7. Count and quality diverge: more options can lower average quality while raising the best option, and a
   few distinct alternatives can outperform many similar ones
   [delmissier-option-generation-ideation-2015]{459} [siebert-novices-press-2016]{454}.
8. Strategy tables organize alternatives as coherent one-per-element selections named by themes, a
   structure prescribed for producing distinct strategies, but without attested controlled evaluation
   [usfws-usgs-sdm-developing-alternatives-2011]{458} [howard-decision-analysis-practice-promise-1988]{457}
   {confidence: abstract-only}.

## Revisit if

- The full text of Siebert & Keeney (2015) is acquired: the five study designs, stimuli conditions,
  quality measures and effect sizes would replace press-release figures and could change section 1.1.
- Gettys, Pliske, Manning & Casey (1987) is read directly: its option-tree completeness measure and the
  overconfidence-in-completeness result are held as cite-through only.
- Controlled evidence appears for strategy tables or value-focused brainstorming versus unstructured
  generation.
- Replications of the master-list paradigm with non-student populations, organizational decisions, or
  machine generators report recall figures outside the 30–50% band.
- A study separates distinctness from count in alternative sets with a controlled manipulation.
