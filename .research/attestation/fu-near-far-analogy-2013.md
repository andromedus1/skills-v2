# The Meaning of "Near" and "Far": The Impact of Structuring Design Databases and the Effect of Distance of Analogy on Design Output (Fu, Chan, Cagan, Kotovsky, Schunn & Wood)

## Source structure and scope
Katherine Fu, Joel Chan, Jonathan Cagan, Kenneth Kotovsky, Christian Schunn, Kristin Wood. JMD 135(2),
021007, February 2013 (publisher PDF via OSF). Read: abstract, §1 introduction, §2.3 participants/procedure,
§3 metrics, §4 results, §5 discussion, §6 conclusions.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** near/far patent stimuli were chosen from a structure built with "latent semantic analysis and a
  Bayesian based algorithm for discovering structural form"; results "confirm that 'near' and 'far' are
  relative terms"; "although the literature has shown that 'far' analogical stimuli are more likely to lead to
  the generation of innovative solutions with novel characteristics, there is such a thing as too far. That is,
  if the stimuli are too distant, they then can become harmful to the design process."
- **§1:** literature review (as reported): Dahl & Moreau found number of far-field analogies positively related
  to originality; random stimuli are sometimes recommended as "extremely far-field"; other work questions far
  analogies — they "can be hard to conceive as relevant" and are "cognitively challenging to retrieve from
  memory"; "a cohesive theory on the effect of analogical distance on design outcomes does not currently exist",
  partly because "near" and "far" lack standard meaning; usefulness "could vary in a curvilinear fashion ...
  with very near and very far stimuli being less helpful (e.g., due to low novelty at the near end and low
  relevance at the far end)." Names design-by-analogy methods: Synectics, inspiration from nature, the WordTree
  method, biomimetic concept generation.
- **§2.3:** 72 engineering students (24 near, 24 far, 24 control); problem: low-cost device generating energy
  from human motion; phases of ideation with stimuli shown after an initial 10-minute phase.
- **§4 results:** distance affected maximum novelty (F(2,59) = 4.139, p = 0.02): far-condition best concepts
  "significantly less novel" than near (d = −0.82) or control (d = −0.91); no significant effect on mean novelty.
  Mean quality: near > far (d = 0.76, p = 0.02); maximum quality: far < near (d = −0.76). Participants rated far
  stimuli "significantly less relevant" (d = −0.64). These results are "contradictory to those found in our
  previous work" (Chan et al. 2011), where far stimuli correlated with higher maximum novelty.
- **§5:** mapping both studies' stimuli into one structure showed the earlier study's "near" patents were
  closer to the problem than its "far" ones and the current "near" patents were not as near as the earlier
  "far" ones — interpreted as "a 'sweet spot' for distance ... 'too near' and 'too far'"; the findings
  "directly challenge the random inspiration methods" since random stimuli "may be too distant ... to allow
  for mapping or transfer"; the sweet spot "is dependent upon and specific to the patents and design problem,
  and number of patents used in this study"; its stabilisation "is an area for future work."
- **§5 on retrieval:** most-used method for searching patents for analogies is keyword search; "retrieval of
  far-field analogies is cognitively difficult" and "remindings tend to be constrained by surface similarity",
  so the probability of retrieving surface-dissimilar analogies unaided is low; proposes computational tools
  to find sweet-spot analogies; suggests clustering patents by functional similarity to surface overlooked
  functional principles, linking representation change to overcoming functional fixedness.
