# Schema Induction and Analogical Transfer (Gick & Holyoak)

## Source structure and scope
Mary L. Gick and Keith J. Holyoak (University of Michigan). Cognitive Psychology 15, 1–38 (1983); scanned copy,
38 pages. Read: abstract, introduction (summary of the 1980 radiation-problem findings), theoretical analysis of
retrieval cues and schemas, Part I overview, Part II Experiment 4 (two analogs, schema quality).

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** predicts analogies "will be noticed on the basis of semantic retrieval cues and that the induction
  of a general schema from concrete analogs will facilitate analogical transfer"; Part I tried to foster schema
  abstraction from a single story via "summarization instructions, a verbal statement of the underlying principle,
  or a diagrammatic representation" — "None of these devices achieved a notable degree of sucess [sic]"; Part II:
  "if two prior analogs were given, subjects often derived a problem schema as an incidental product of describing
  the similarities of the analogs"; "quality of the induced schema was highly predictive of subsequent transfer";
  verbal statements and diagrams that failed with one analog "proved highly beneficial when paired with two."
- **Introduction (pp.4–5), summarising the authors' earlier radiation-problem work:** with a hint to use the
  prior military story, "about 75%" produced the convergence solution vs "less than 10%" of controls; without a
  hint "only about 30%" did; "only a third or less of the subjects who could potentially apply the analogy
  spontaneously noticed it"; "It is thus no easy matter to spontaneously notice an analogy between two
  superficially dissimilar situations, even in our highly simplified experimental paradigm."
- **Experiment 4 (Table 5, p.22–23):** convergence solution before hint — two similar analogs 39%, two dissimilar
  analogs 52%, one analog plus control story 21%; two-analog conditions 45% vs 21% before hint (G² = 6.35,
  p < .01), and 80% vs 53% in total; "two analogs were better than one."
- **Schema quality (Table 6, p.24):** 21% good, 20% intermediate, 59% poor schemas; poor schemas included
  similarities "abstract to the point of vacuity (e.g., 'In both stories a problem was solved using logical
  means')"; 91% of good-schema subjects solved without a hint vs 30% of poor-schema subjects; "One must be
  cautious in suggesting a causal basis" since skilled solvers may both write good schemas and transfer well.
