# Do the best design ideas (really) come from conceptually distant sources of inspiration? (Chan, Dow & Schunn)

## Source structure and scope
Joel Chan (Pittsburgh LRDC), Steven P. Dow (CMU HCII), Christian D. Schunn (Pittsburgh LRDC).
Design Studies, in-press author copy (2014 copyright line; DOI 10.1016/j.destud.2014.08.001), 33-page
PDF obtained via the senior author's OSF-hosted publication list. Read in full: abstract, §1 background,
§2 method (OpenIDEO data, LDA distance measure, validation), §3 results, §4 discussion and caveats.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** tests "the popular but unevenly supported hypothesis that conceptually distant sources of
  inspiration provide the best insights for creative production"; across "hundreds of design concepts across a
  dozen different design challenges" on a web innovation platform, "citing sources is associated with greater
  creativity of ideas, but conceptually closer rather than farther sources appear more beneficial", and the
  "inverse relationship ... is robust across different design problems on the platform."
- **§1.1:** names the claim that far sources are best the "Conceptual Leap Hypothesis", supported by anecdotes
  (Kekulé, Velcro) and case studies; "empirical support for this proposal is mixed" — some studies found far
  sources better for novelty/quality/flexibility, others found equivalence, and "Fu et al. (2013) even found
  that far sources led to lower novelty and quality of ideas than near sources" (as reported by these authors).
- **§1.2 (methodological critique of prior work):** prior studies "may be too short (typically 30 min to 1 h)
  to convert far sources into viable concepts"; "many far sources may yield shallow or unusable inferences",
  so "designers might have to sift through many samples of far sources to find 'hidden gems'"; most
  experiments have small cells (N ≤ 12 per treatment), and "only four studies had N ≥ 18 ... and they are
  evenly split in support/opposition for the benefits of far sources"; problem variation: Chiu & Shu (2012)
  "observed inconsistent effects across problems" (as reported).
- **§2 data:** 2341 concepts across 12 OpenIDEO challenges; 707 concepts cited inspirations and form the
  analysis sample; 110 (~16%) were shortlisted. Creativity operationalised as expert-panel shortlisting.
  Conceptual distance = cosine distance from the challenge brief using a 400-topic LDA model, validated against
  human similarity judgments (ICC(2,5) = 0.74 for the human ratings).
- **§3.2.1:** a 1-unit increase in maximum source distance (DISTMAX) "predicted a 0.33 decrease in the log-odds
  of being shortlisted" (95% CI −0.06 to −0.60), controlling for feedback, cited-shortlisted sources and
  author/challenge nesting; "the 'true' coefficient is not positive (contra the Conceptual Leap Hypothesis), but
  we are quite uncertain about its magnitude." The advantage of citing vs not citing "seems to be driven mostly
  by citing relatively near inspirations: Pr(shortlist) for concepts that cite far inspirations converges on
  that of no-citation concepts." Essentially zero between-challenge variation in slope.
- **§3.2.2:** mean distance (DISTMEAN) coefficient ≈ −0.40 log-odds, p < .05, same pattern.
- **§4.1:** "no support for the notion that the best ideas come from building explicitly on the farthest
  sources."
- **§4.2 caveats:** conditional on explicitly cited sources — "silent on the effects of distance for concepts
  that did not cite sources"; possible range restriction from self-identified sources; novelty was not measured
  separately — "the benefits of distance may only be best observed for the novelty of ideas, and not necessarily
  quality, consistent with some recent work (Franke, Poetz, & Schreier, 2014)", but the authors argue creativity
  needs novelty and quality together.
- **§4.3:** results "lend strength to alternative theories" (Perkins: distance does not matter; Weisberg:
  within-domain expertise drives creativity); "some creative ideas did come from far sources", but the most
  creative ideas "are more likely to come from relying on a preponderance of nearer rather than farther sources";
  good ideas "may arise from iterative, deep search".
