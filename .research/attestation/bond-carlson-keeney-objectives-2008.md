# Generating Objectives: Can Decision Makers Articulate What They Want? (Bond, Carlson & Keeney 2008)

## Source structure and scope
Samuel D. Bond, Kurt A. Carlson, Ralph L. Keeney. Management Science 54(1), January 2008, pp. 56–70,
doi 10.1287/mnsc.1070.0754. Three empirical studies (MBA / executive-MBA students; MBA program choice
and summer-internship choice) plus a real-world case study (Seagate Software, 12 executives).
Engagement depth: full text read (introduction, methods, results, case study, discussion, limitations).

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** "In three empirical studies, participants consistently omitted nearly half of the
  objectives that they later identified as personally relevant. More surprisingly, omitted objectives
  were perceived to be almost as important as those generated by participants on their own." Replicated
  in a strategic case study at a high-tech firm.
- **§2 (paradigm):** generate-then-recognize design. Participants (1) list all relevant objectives
  unaided; (2) check relevant items on an experimenter-built "master list"; (3) map own items to master
  list; objectives checked but not mapped are "recognized objectives" (omitted at generation); (4) rank
  or rate importance. The master list is the completeness reference.
- **§3.1.2 (Study 1 master list construction):** 28 items "created by the experimenters through
  brainstorming, internal discussion, and communication with prior MBA students."
- **§3.2 (Study 1, n=62 usable of 84):** mean 7.4 objectives listed; 13.6 checked on master list;
  recognized 7.6 vs self-generated 6.0 (paired t(61)=2.03, p<0.05). 10% did not generate their most
  important objective; 71% overlooked at least one of their top five.
- **§3.3 (effort explanation tested):** correlation between number self-generated and number recognized
  was slightly positive (r=0.18, ns), not the negative correlation an inadequate-effort account
  predicts; authors attribute omissions to "an incomplete cognitive representation of the decision."
- **§4 (Study 2, executive MBA, n=70 usable):** master list expanded to 31 by adding popular "written-in"
  items from Study 1; listed 5.9; checked 14.3; recognized 7.7 vs self-generated 6.6 (ns). After a one-week
  delay before ranking, 35% did not generate their most important objective; 79% had at least one
  recognized objective in their top five; mean rank of recognized 5.8 vs self-generated 5.1 (p<0.04).
- **§5 (Study 3, prospective internship decision, n=28):** participants asked to ruminate over one week
  with a mid-week reminder and encouraged to consult others; master list of 29 objectives built from
  participant responses plus the authors' analyses. Checked 14.8; self-generated 6.8 vs recognized 7.9.
  Importance (1–9): recognized 6.8 vs self-generated 7.4 (t(27)=3.98, p<0.01); bogus objective 2.9;
  baseline objective 4.7.
- **§5.3 (mapping bias):** participants mapped liberally (e.g. "multicultural and multidisciplinary group
  of coworkers" mapped to "interact with senior managers"); the authors judge "the mapping procedure
  overstates the completeness of participants' self-generated lists."
- **§6 (Seagate case):** one-hour value-focused probing interviews with 12 executives (prompts by
  stakeholder category: customers, shareholders, society, personal objectives), then written feedback
  and invitation to add. Aggregate: 39 specific objectives in 8 categories; individuals listed on
  average 14.17 (36%); only 1 of 12 listed more than half. "Collectively, the aggregated list ... was a
  much more complete set." Managers reached consensus the aggregate represented what the firm wanted.
- **§7:** "merely considering the decision at multiple points in time is insufficient to redress the
  problem"; external assistance appears required.
- **§7.1 (limitations):** "This research offers no direct evidence that the inadequacies ... would
  degrade the quality of eventual decisions."
- **§7.2:** unaided listing "is an insufficient method for eliciting a complete set of objectives";
  overlooked means objectives hamper creation of alternatives. Master lists compiled from many
  individuals are linked to benefits of aggregating group responses.
