# Marginality and Problem Solving Effectiveness in Broadcast Search (Jeppesen & Lakhani)

## Source structure and scope
Lars Bo Jeppesen (Copenhagen Business School), Karim R. Lakhani (Harvard Business School). Accepted
manuscript (September 18, 2009) in Harvard's DASH repository, 45 pages. Read: abstract, §1, theory/hypotheses,
data and sample description, results (Model 2–4), limitations, §8 discussion. Disclosure on p.1: Lakhani
joined InnoCentive's Scientific Advisory Board after data collection and analysis.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** winners in science problem-solving contests with "open broadcast of problem information,
  self-selection of external solvers ... and blind review"; dataset of "166 science challenges involving over
  12,000 scientists"; "technical and social marginality, being a source of different perspectives and
  heuristics, plays an important role"; "The provision of a winning solution was positively related to
  increasing distance between the solver's field of technical expertise and the focal field of the problem";
  female solvers "performed significantly better than men".
- **Theory (pp.11–12):** marginal solvers have "focused naïveté"; "Any given new perspective is not likely to be
  useful for solving a difficult problem: what matters is to be different and relevant (Page 2007)"; "the odds
  that any given solver will be able to solve a specific problem are quite low; ... those odds increase with each
  additional solver who arrives with a different analytical toolkit ... This is precisely the mechanism behind
  broadcast search". H1: success positively associated with distance between solver's field and the problem's.
- **Sample (pp.23–25):** problems from 26 firms' R&D labs posted on InnoCentive; 49 of 166 solved (29.5% solve
  rate); on average 240.7 people opened each problem statement and 9.9 submitted; solved problems received more
  than twice the submissions (16.8 vs 7.0). In one case four winning solutions to one challenge (food-grade
  polymer delivery) came from an aerospace physicist, an agribusiness owner, a transdermal drug-delivery
  specialist and an industrial scientist, "with differing scientific mechanisms". Survey: 320 complete responses
  from 993 submitters (32.2%), covering 93 of 166 problems.
- **Results (p.30):** for each increment on the self-assessed "Problem-Expertise Distance" scale, "the odds of
  creating a winning solution increase by 3.3% (significant at the 10% level with a standard error of 0.017)";
  female solvers' probability of winning higher by 23.4% (1% level).
- **Limitations (pp.31–32):** technical marginality is a self-assessed distance, open to accuracy and field-status
  errors; winner selection "might be biased towards 'out-of-field' solutions" because firms had already failed
  internally; problems broadcast may be "non-strategic or externally solvable", so extrapolation to all problem
  types needs caution.
- **§8 (p.34):** "we urge caution in extrapolating the technical marginality finding to the extreme"; "one might
  anticipate an 'inverse-U' relationship"; the finding "is conditional on self-selection by solvers" — those who
  judged themselves very far "may never have participated." Successful solvers "used 'local' knowledge to
  formulate a solution to a problem in a relatively distant field."
