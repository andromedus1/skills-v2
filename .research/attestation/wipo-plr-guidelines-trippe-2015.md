# Guidelines for Preparing Patent Landscape Reports (WIPO, 2015)

## Source structure and scope
Guidelines prepared for the World Intellectual Property Organization by Anthony Trippe
(Patinformatics, LLC) with contributions from the WIPO Secretariat, 2015; 131-page PDF.
Developed under WIPO Development Agenda project DA_19_30_31_02 (Ch. 1). Purpose stated as
instructing new practitioners on the stages of a patent landscape report (PLR) and helping
continue the development of standards and best practices (Ch. 1). Engagement depth: full
text extracted; chapters 1, 4.2.1.5, 7.4, 8.1.3, 8.2, 8.3.3, 8.3.5, 8.7 and Annex ToR
section 3.4 read closely; other chapters skimmed via contents. It is a practitioner
guideline, not an empirical evaluation; no measured comparison of methods is reported.

## Anchored details
- **§8.1.3 Defining the scope (pp. 74-75):** more than 60 million patent documents exist;
  looking at all on a topic is "trying to boil the ocean". Scope gives "focus and direction"
  and, if "agreed upon by all the stakeholders", tells readers the constraints of the
  analysis. "In any given technical area there are likely to be many different approaches to
  addressing a particular issue"; with large topics "it is good practice to limit a PLR to a
  handful of the most important technical approaches". Scope should also state explicitly
  what is out of scope, plus countries, time period, family-reduction method and whether
  non-patent literature is included.
- **§7.3 / §8.2 (p. 81-82) "Linear Law of Patent Analysis":** "the question drives the data";
  each step builds on the previous one; agreement on topic and scope makes search parameters
  straightforward.
- **§8.2 "reverse engineering" search (p. 82):** a quick title-field search yields a few
  highly on-target references; these are "reverse-engineered" to identify relevant
  classification codes, database indexing and inventor terms; a broader search follows and
  its results are "examined for new synonyms, and additional codes. The process is repeated
  until no additional search items are identified, and the analyst feels they have conducted
  a thorough search." (The stopping rule is saturation of new search terms/codes plus analyst
  judgement.)
- **§7.4 Precision and recall (pp. 68-69):** recall = how much of the useful information the
  search retrieved; precision = how much retrieved is useful; normally opposed. For PLRs "it
  might be more productive to begin with creating sets using methods that produce high recall
  exclusive of precision"; macro-level statistics see only major trends, so some imprecision
  is tolerable; significant trends should be checked to ensure they come from precise
  references.
- **§8.2 (p. 82):** "if the level of recall can be established at higher than 90%, while the
  precision kept above 70%", off-topic items are unlikely to distort the analysis; "Major
  statistical finding should always be validated". The passages read do not specify a
  procedure for establishing the recall figure.
- **§8.3.3 (p. 87):** sometimes impossible to reach 70% precision by query; smaller
  collections may need ~90%; manual review of "false drops" is then required.
- **§8.2.1 (p. 83):** searching some fields (life sciences) requires subject-matter experts
  and professional searchers; the Atazanavir example shows a compound searched under
  pre-clinical names, generic names and Markush/substructure methods because the name used
  at filing differed from later names (a vocabulary-over-time problem).
- **§4.2.1.5 Classifications (p. 21):** classification codes "represent predefined concepts for
  describing the technical features"; often narrow, so broader analyses aggregate a range of
  codes; IPC applies to almost all jurisdictions, CPC only to some; documents carry multiple
  codes and database ordering can bias analyses that take only the first code.
- **§8.3.5 Generating technology categories (pp. 89-90):** categories "are sometimes
  identified using the patent data itself, for instance, with classification codes, but
  ideally they should be generated based on input from a subject-matter expert based on an
  industry standard view". Classification codes rarely match market categories ("it is a
  happy accident when they align with the business needs"); offices differ in systems and
  granularity. Methods named: classification codes; one search strategy per category (works
  best when categories are clearly distinct); categories created "on the fly" during manual
  review in a spreadsheet, naming a new category "when the analyst comes across a new
  approach", often needing a second pass to reclassify; semi/fully automated ML or semantic
  grouping. Analyst must decide single- vs multi-category membership and state it.
- **Example (p. 89):** solar cooking PLR segmented into complete systems vs components, then
  grouped by heating technique (reflected concentration, trapped heating, indirect heating,
  direct absorption) and sub-grouped by component.
- **§8.7 (p. 103):** a different analyst should be able to reproduce the results given the
  same data; methodology and limitations sections are required.
- **Annex ToR §3.4 (p. 126):** reports must narrate how queries were developed and refined,
  how classification symbols were identified and how useful they were, how citations were
  exploited; discuss "acceptable tolerances with respect to recall and precision"; present
  the effect of adding CPC and FI to "improve recall in comparison to purely IPC based
  searches"; explain other recall strategies such as supplementary keyword searches or
  citation analysis; "Numerical evidence should be included."
- **Ch. 12 literature list (p. 117):** names Bubela et al., "Patent landscaping for life
  sciences innovation: toward consistent and transparent practices", Nature Biotechnology 31,
  202-206, 2013.
