# Option generation in decision making: ideation beyond memory retrieval (Del Missier, Visentini & Mäntylä 2015)

## Source structure and scope
Fabio Del Missier, Mimì Visentini, Timo Mäntylä. Frontiers in Psychology 5:1584, published 22 January
2015, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01584. Literature review of option-generation research plus an
individual-differences study (N=141 undergraduates; three ill-structured problems: city parking, non-profit
fund raising, domestic energy saving; 6 minutes each). Engagement depth: full text read (introduction,
review, methods, results, discussion through applied implications).

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** option generation fluency and diversity "are supported by ideation ability even after
  taking into account" episodic/semantic memory, cognitive control and other measures; "generating more
  options may have multifaceted consequences for choice, increasing the quality of the best option
  generated but decreasing the mean quality of the options in the generated set."
- **Review, p.2:** "huge individual differences in the number of generated options" (attributed to
  Manning et al. 1980; Engelmann & Gettys 1985; Gettys et al. 1987); domain expertise "does not play a
  significant role, at least in the majority of investigations" (attributed to Mehle 1982; Klein et al.
  1995; contrary cases Engelmann & Gettys 1985; Ward et al. 2011).
- **Review, p.3 ("less is more" vs "quantity breeds quality"):** Johnson & Raab (2003), handball players
  under time constraints, are reported as finding average option-set quality declines as more options are
  generated, quality declines with generation order, and generating more options led to less consistent
  and poorer choices ("but see Ward et al., 2011, for contrasting findings"). Gettys et al. (1987) and
  Klein et al. (1995) are reported as showing that "at least some good options are usually reported."
- **Review, p.3 (departures from optimal generation):** suboptimal generation reported for a campus
  parking problem and housing for an impecunious friend (Gettys et al. 1987), uses for a geographic area
  (Manning et al. 1980), expert mechanics (Mehle 1982), experienced chess players (Klein et al. 1995);
  "replicated even when providing very ample generation time or incentives for the quality or quantity of
  generated options (Gettys et al., 1987), suggesting a cognitively-driven difficulty."
- **Footnote 3, p.3:** related difficulty in accessing alternatives in fault-tree research (Fischhoff et
  al. 1978), predecisional focusing, and part-set cuing; "Participants are also usually overconfident
  about the completeness of the option generation performance: They think they have generated a large
  fraction of the potential options, even if they have identified only a limited part of the existing
  possibilities (Mehle, 1982; Gettys et al., 1987)."
- **Review, p.3 (ideation strategies named):** leverage points (Klein & Wolf 1998); lateral vs vertical
  transformations (Goel 2010); focusing on different structural aspects of the problem (Keller & Ho
  1988; Keeney 1994, 1999); avoiding idea blocking; analogical reasoning and perspective change.
- **Methods, p.5 (diversity measure):** diversity = number of distinct predefined response categories
  accessed, "akin to the 'limb' of the option trees in Gettys et al., 1987"; categories built from prior
  studies and domain literature and refined after a pilot (e.g. parking: use space more efficiently,
  increase space, reduce demand, other). Two raters; classification kappa > 0.85; quality ratings r > 0.70.
- **Footnote 7, p.8:** participants who accessed more response categories also generated more options
  (r = 0.52, 0.45, 0.66).
- **Results, Tables 1–2:** ideation fluency (alternative uses) added ΔR² of 0.193 (parking), 0.141 (fund
  raising), 0.168 (energy) to option-generation fluency beyond basic cognitive measures, verbal fluency
  and knowledge/experience; basic measures predicted the Applying Decision Rules task instead.
- **Results, Table 3 and p.10:** fluency correlated negatively with mean quality (controlling max quality)
  in parking (−0.18) and fund raising (−0.21), and positively with max quality in fund raising (0.27) and
  energy (0.31), not parking (0.05). Choice quality related to both mean and max quality. Fluency–choice
  quality correlations all non-significant; indirect effects "of small magnitude." Summary: "higher
  fluency and diversity had both positive and negative effects ... moderated by the problem."
- **Discussion, p.12:** reliance on associative retrieval vs ideation may depend on problem familiarity;
  ideation plays a greater role "in non-routinary contexts."
