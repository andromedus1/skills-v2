# A Study of Design Fixation, Its Mitigation and Perception in Engineering Design Faculty (Linsey, Tseng, Fu, Cagan, Wood & Schunn)

## Source structure and scope
J. S. Linsey, I. Tseng, K. Fu, J. Cagan, K. L. Wood, C. Schunn. JMD 132, 041003, April 2010 (publisher PDF via
OSF). Read: abstract, §2 background (fixation, perception of fixation), §4 method, §5 metrics, §6–§9 results,
§10–11 perceptions and research questions.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** compares "a control, a fixation group whom were provided with an example solution, and a
  defixation group whom were also given materials to mitigate their design fixation"; engineering design faculty
  "show statistically significant evidence of design fixation, but only partially perceive its effects"; "design
  fixation can be mitigated"; "designers, even those that study and teach design on a regular basis, do not know
  when they are being influenced or fixated by misleading or poor information."
- **§2 (as reported from prior literature):** Dahl & Moreau found "subjects exposed to within-domain examples
  employed fewer far-domain analogies" and originality increased when subjects were encouraged to use analogies
  and given no example; Marsh et al. found within-domain examples biased toward similar features; Ward and
  co-workers found that when asked to avoid producing solutions similar to examples, similarity "did not
  significantly decrease"; Tseng et al. found surface-dissimilar but relevant information "only affects problem
  solving when the designer has already begun work on the problem"; "Common solutions to design problems were
  shown to create greater fixation (fewer total solutions) than unusual solutions."
- **§4:** problem — device to shell peanuts for use in places like Haiti/West Africa. Fixation group got a
  deliberately flawed gasoline-press example. Defixation group got "alternative representations of the problem"
  — "a brief functional description, useful analogies, a list of available energy sources, and a quick
  back-of-the-envelope calculation"; some analogies found with the WordTree method via WordNet hypernyms and
  troponyms of "remove" and "shell". 34 attendees (mostly faculty) at an NSF workshop; analysed n = 9 control,
  12 fixation, 10 defixation; 45 minutes ideation.
- **§6 results:** number of non-redundant ideas differed (F = 3.7, p < 0.04); control > fixation (t = 2.94,
  p < 0.02); defixation trended above fixation. Feature reuse differed (Kruskal–Wallis H = 7.3, p = 0.03);
  fixation used more example features than control (p = 0.02) and than defixation (p = 0.08).
- **§7:** fixation group produced a larger percentage of gas-powered designs (randomisation ANOVA p = 0.05);
  defixation vs fixation trend (p < 0.08).
- **§8 summary:** defixation materials "did assist in reducing their fixation ... but it did not completely
  eliminate it. Participants in the control group still outperformed both the fixation and the defixation group
  in total number of concepts, whereas the defixation group employed a greater diversity of energy-based
  solutions."
- **§9:** number of self-reported analogies did not differ across conditions (H = 1.4, p = 0.5).
- **§10:** the fixation group tended to believe the example had influenced them positively, in contrast with
  measured negative effects; "participant perceptions are not an accurate tool for evaluating design methods."
