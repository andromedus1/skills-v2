# Quantifying biodiversity: procedures and pitfalls in the measurement and comparison of species richness

## Source structure and scope
Nicholas J. Gotelli (University of Vermont) and Robert K. Colwell (University of Connecticut).
*Ecology Letters* (2001) 4: 379–391 (as printed). Review of sampling pitfalls in comparing taxon richness:
individual- vs sample-based accumulation and rarefaction curves, species density vs richness, taxonomic
(category–subcategory) ratios, and asymptotic richness estimators. Engagement depth: full text read.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** ecologists "have not always appreciated the effects of abundance and sampling effort on
  richness measures"; pitfalls "can be largely avoided by using accumulation and rarefaction curves";
  "Asymptotic richness estimators provide lower-bound estimates for taxon-rich groups such as tropical
  arthropods, in which observed richness rarely reaches an asymptote, despite intensive sampling."
- **Taxon sampling curves:** the curve "rises relatively rapidly at first, then much more slowly in later
  samples as increasingly rare taxa are added"; four kinds of curve from two dichotomies; the unit of
  replication (individual vs sample of individuals) is "a distinction that turns out to be far from trivial".
- **Comparison rule:** "Raw species richness counts or higher taxon counts can be validly compared only when
  taxon accumulation curves have reached a clear asymptote"; for invertebrate, microbial and many tropical
  taxa "such asymptotes may never be reached"; then the curves themselves may be compared after scaling.
- **Category–subcategory ratios (Figs 5–6):** "the number of genera reaches an asymptote sooner than the number
  of species. This pattern is inevitable for any two taxonomic ranks" because the higher rank has fewer members;
  subtaxon–taxon ratios "are an increasing function of sample size", so small samples show fewer species per
  genus regardless of biology (Williams; Simberloff, as cited).
- **Asymptotic estimators section:** rarefaction "cannot be used for extrapolation" (Tipper 1979, as cited).
  Nonparametric estimators based on mark–recapture statistics use singletons, doubletons and other rare species:
  "The greater the number of rare species in a dataset, the more likely it is that other species are present that
  were not represented in the dataset." Estimators are tested on exhaustively censused benchmark datasets; an
  ideal estimator would reach its own asymptote much sooner than the rarefaction curve and approximate the
  empirical asymptote without bias.
- **Caveat:** "The tricky issue is whether the performance of the estimators on benchmark datasets – which
  usually consist of relatively small numbers of species – accurately predicts the performance ... on
  not-yet-asymptotic datasets"; for hyperdiverse taxa estimators "often fail to reach any asymptote at all,
  rising more or less in parallel with the still-steep sample-based rarefaction curve", and "must be viewed as
  providing only lower-bound estimates" (attributed to Anne Chao, personal communication). Restricting to
  ecologically more homogeneous subsets of samples "sometimes does produce well-behaved, asymptotic richness
  estimates" (Longino et al., in press, as cited).
