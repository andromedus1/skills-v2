# Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena

## Source structure and scope
Lianmin Zheng, Wei-Lin Chiang, Ying Sheng, Siyuan Zhuang and coauthors (UC Berkeley,
UC San Diego, Carnegie Mellon University, Stanford, MBZUAI). arXiv 2306.05685 v4,
24 Dec 2023; arXiv comments field reads "NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track".
Read via the arXiv HTML rendering: abstract, introduction, limitations (3.3),
mitigations (3.4), agreement setup and results (4.1, 4.2). Appendix tables were not audited.
Judges studied are 2023-era models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude-v1, Vicuna); tasks are
open-ended chat questions (MT-bench, 80 questions) and Chatbot Arena votes.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** the paper examines "position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases, as
  well as limited reasoning ability" of LLM judges and reports that strong judges such as
  GPT-4 achieve "over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans."
- **Section 3.3, "Self-enhancement bias":** defined as "the effect that LLM judges may favor
  the answers generated by themselves." Observational result against human win rates:

  > For example, GPT-4 favors itself with a 10% higher win rate; Claude-v1 favors itself with a 25% higher win rate. However, they also favor other models and GPT-3.5 does not favor itself.

  The authors then qualify it:

  > Due to limited data and small differences, our study cannot determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias.

  They add that a controlled study is hard because a response cannot easily be restyled
  as another model's without changing quality.
- **Section 3.3, "Limited capability in grading math and reasoning questions":** GPT-4 can
  misgrade even basic math it can itself solve, being "influenced by the given answers"
  (Figure 12 caption).
- **Section 3.4, "Chain-of-thought and reference-guided judge":** with chain-of-thought the
  judge "in many cases ... makes exactly the same mistake as the given answers"; a
  reference-guided judge (judge answers independently first, then grades against that
  reference) reduced the math-grading failure rate "from 70% to 15%" over the default prompt
  (Table 4).
- **Section 4.1, setup:** MT-bench answers from 6 models graded by LLM judges and
  "58 expert-level human labelers" (mostly graduate students), about 3K votes; Chatbot Arena
  sample of 3K crowd votes. Agreement is "the probability of randomly selected individuals
  (but not identical) of each type agreeing on a randomly selected question."
- **Section 4.2, "High agreement between GPT-4 and humans":**

  > The agreement under setup S2 (w/o tie) between GPT-4 and humans reaches 85%, which is even higher than the agreement among humans (81%).

  Setup S2 counts only non-tie votes (Table 5 caption; random-judge baseline R = 50%).
  When humans disagreed with GPT-4 and were shown its judgment, they "deemed GPT-4's
  judgments reasonable in 75% of cases and are even willing to change their choices in 34%
  of cases." Agreement rises "from 70% to nearly 100%" as the performance gap between the
  compared models grows (Table 7), i.e. agreement is lowest where the compared answers are
  close in quality.
