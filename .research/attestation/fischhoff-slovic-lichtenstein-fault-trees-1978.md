# Fault Trees: Sensitivity of Estimated Failure Probabilities to Problem Representation (Fischhoff, Slovic & Lichtenstein 1978)

## Source structure and scope
Baruch Fischhoff, Paul Slovic, Sarah Lichtenstein (Decision Research). Journal of Experimental
Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 4(2), 1978, pp. 330–344. Six experiments varying the
structure of a fault tree for "a car fails to start"; subjects were college students and, in Experiment 6,
experienced garage mechanics. Engagement depth: abstract, Experiments 1, 2 and 6, and General Discussion
read; Experiments 3–5 (detail/splitting manipulations) skimmed.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** "(a) People were quite insensitive to what had been left out of a fault tree, (b)
  increasing the amount of detail ... produced small effects on perceptions, and (c) the perceived
  importance of a particular branch was increased by presenting it in pieces." Insensitivity to omissions
  "was found with both college student subjects and experienced garage mechanics."
- **Experiment 1 (pruning):** subjects saw either the full tree or a pruned tree with major branches
  removed, each with an "all other problems" branch. For pruned tree Group 1, "other" "should have
  increased by a factor of six (from .078 to .468)" to absorb the removed branches; "Instead, 'other' was
  only doubled," while the importance of the branches shown increased. Some sensitivity remained: "other"
  was significantly larger for pruned groups (p < .001).
- **Experiment 2 (focusing on "all other"):** explicitly directing attention to what the "other" category
  contains raised pruned-tree "other" by about 50% (Group 1: .140 to .217; Group 2: .227 to .346), "still
  much less than it should have been." Discussion: "Focusing subjects' attention on what is missing
  improved their awareness, but only partially," and did not cause exaggerated estimates of what was
  missing.
- **Experiment 6 (experts):** 29 experienced mechanics (2–43 years). Pruned-tree mechanics assigned
  "other" a mean of .215 where the omitted branches plus other summed to .441 (medians .16 vs .47).
  Self-rated knowledge did not track sensitivity (much-above-average mechanics assigned .075). The
  section cites Fischhoff (1977) as listing incidents in which expert fault-tree designers "were apparently
  unaware of major omissions; they therefore greatly overestimated the exhaustiveness of their own
  analyses."
- **General Discussion:** "The most dramatic result ... was subjects' inability to appreciate how much had
  been omitted from the pruned fault trees. Exaggeration of fault tree completeness was found with both
  'regular' subjects and experienced mechanics." Explanation favoured: availability ("what is out of
  sight is also out of mind") over a credibility account; listed sources of expert omission include
  unimagined human error, unexamined assumptions about constancies, and overconfidence in current
  knowledge.
