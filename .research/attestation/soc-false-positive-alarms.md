# 99% False Positives: A Qualitative Study of SOC Analysts' Perspectives on Security Alarms

## Source structure and scope
Bushra A. Alahmadi, Louise Axon, Ivan Martinovic (University of Oxford). The USENIX presentation page lists it in
the 31st USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security 22), 2022, pp. 2783-2800. Mixed-method: an online survey of
security-operations-center practitioners (n = 20) used to focus semi-structured interviews (n = 21, seven SOCs,
mostly UK/Europe). Qualitative findings; survey responses are Likert counts, not measured alarm rates.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** "despite the perceived volume of FPs, most are attributed to benign triggers—true alarms, explained
  by legitimate behavior in the organization’s environment, which analysts may choose to ignore." "Alarm validation
  is a tedious task that can cause alarm burnout and eventually desensitization."
- **Section 4 (survey), Table 3:** A-1 "The monitoring tools I use frequently produce false positive results"
  (Disagree:Agree 2:14); A-4 "The number of alerts generated by most IDS are overwhelming" (4:12). Of analysts who
  shared counts, "one participant indicated that one out of every 100 alerts investigated is an actual threat, while
  another stated that in every 200 alerts, 50 were found to be legitimate." "47% said they did so based on the alert
  severity rating" (versus 58% based on awareness of regular network activity).
- **Section 5 (alarm handling):** alarms "are periodically reviewed by the analysts to tune the defined alarms
  further and eliminate FPs"; tuning and base-lining "fall on the analysts themselves".
- **Section 6 "RQ2: Definition of FPs":** B3: "We know 99% of the alarms we generate are false positives, but we
  still have to look at them." The authors distinguish false alarms from "benign triggers" (real matches the
  organization ignores for a "business-justified" purpose) and "noisy" signatures (G19: "they’re always doing exactly
  what they should do"). Summary:

  > When the analyst reported a 99% FP rate, this is found to be mostly benign triggers and not necessarily a measurement of the performance of the technology itself.

- **Section 7 (alarm quality):** "the lack of explainability causes alarm desensitization"; B4: ambiguous
  descriptions lead to "uncertainty about the existence of an attack, resulting in no action from analysts." The
  paper elicits five properties for alarms: Reliable, Explainable, Analytical, Contextual, Transferable (abstract).
- **Section 1 (introduction):** recounts that in the 2013 Target breach a malware alarm "was escalated to the
  Minneapolis SOC, it was ignored, and no action was taken" (citing a news source).
- **Section 9 "Limitations":** "our sample size is small (n = 21)"; seven SOCs; non-random recruitment; "results
  are biased towards this industry/region."
