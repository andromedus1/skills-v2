# Where next for research on fixation, inspiration and creativity in design? (Crilly & Cardoso 2017)

## Source structure and scope
Nathan Crilly (Cambridge) and Carlos Cardoso (TU Delft); Design Studies 50 (2017) 1–38, open access
(CC BY), obtained from the Cambridge repository (bitstream PDF, 38 pages). Report of a one-day
international workshop (Delft, 4 March 2016) with contributors including Steven Smith, Kees Dorst,
Gabriela Goldschmidt, Nigel Cross. Read: introduction, observations, §2.1, §2.4, §2.6–2.7 portions.
Agenda-setting review, not a new experiment.

## Anchored details
- **Introduction (pp.2–3):** design fixation originally "a blind adherence to a set of ideas or concepts
  limiting the output of conceptual design" (Jansson & Smith 1991, p.3, as quoted). Jansson & Smith's
  student designers shown pictures of existing solutions "tended to repeat key features of the solutions
  that were represented. This behaviour persisted even when participants received instructions to avoid
  repeating particular features of those example solutions." Rooted in functional fixedness (Maier,
  Duncker) and the Einstellung effect (Luchins), as reported.
- **p.3:** enough studies exist for dedicated reviews on concepts (Youmans & Arciszewski 2014), findings
  (Sio, Kotovsky & Cagan 2015) and methods (Vasconcelos & Crilly 2016).
- **pp.3–4 five observations:** fixation research is "distinctive", "productive", "disconnected",
  "uncritical" ("seldom been self-critical ... Concerns can be raised over conceptual precision,
  methodological validity and potential impact"), and "undirected".
- **§2.1 working definition:** "design fixation is a state in which someone engaged in a design task
  undertakes a restricted exploration of the design space due to an unconscious bias resulting from prior
  experiences, knowledge or assumptions." Fixation "can be seen as a cognitive 'error' because areas of the
  design space are inadvertently left unexplored"; such errors "might be quite rare". Prior knowledge has
  positive (inspiration) and negative (fixation) consequences.
- **§2.4:** fixation "is identified in the experiments in a relatively consistent manner, as the
  repetition of features from a prior solution"; whether repetition is unintentional "is often not
  assessed"; feature repetition may have benefits ("decreased time, increased confidence and improved
  stakeholder acceptance"); fixation that "encourages a deep exploration of a narrow set of viable
  solutions ... might save time".
- **§2.7 experimental methods:** typical studies use open-ended early ideation problems, basic sketches,
  "limited time ... (often around an hour)", visual example solutions, "inexperienced participants ...
  (typically students)", outcome-focused analysis, and "subjective assessment of design ideas ... (usually
  by researchers)"; raises ecological-validity questions.
- **§2.6:** questions for practice include "What role does design process play in causing or mitigating
  fixation? What role do chance, random occurrences play in comparison to systematic or calculated
  approaches?" and what role groups play "in inducing or overcoming fixation".
