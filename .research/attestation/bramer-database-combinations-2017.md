# Optimal database combinations for literature searches in systematic reviews: a prospective exploratory study

## Source structure and scope
Bramer WM, Rethlefsen ML, Kleijnen J, Franco OH. Systematic Reviews 2017 (DOI
10.1186/s13643-017-0644-y, PMC5718002). Prospective exploratory study of 58 published systematic
reviews whose searches the first author ran. Engagement depth: full text (abstract, methods,
results, strengths and limitations).

## Anchored details
- **Design:** recall, precision and number needed to read computed per database and combination,
  with the denominator being the included references of each published review (1746 relevant
  references found by the database searches; 84 further included references "retrieved by
  other search methods" were excluded from analysis).
- **Results:** "Sixteen percent of the included references (291 articles) were only found in a
  single database"; Embase alone 85.9% overall recall; Embase+MEDLINE 92.8%; Embase, MEDLINE,
  Web of Science and Google Scholar "achieving an overall recall of 98.3 and 100% recall in 72%
  of systematic reviews."
- **Practice estimate:** "We estimate that 60% of published systematic reviews do not retrieve
  95% of all available relevant references as many fail to search important databases" - "with
  the generous assumption that the searches in those databases had been designed sensitively
  enough."
- **Limitations:** did not examine whether lost references changed review conclusions; single
  experienced searcher; "our search strategies, like any other search strategy, still missed
  some relevant references because relevant terms had not been used in the search"; "We are not
  implying that a combined search of the four recommended databases will never result in
  relevant references being missed".
- **Strength claim:** prior work mostly measured whether included references "could have
  theoretically been found" (coverage) rather than what searches actually retrieved.
