# Systematic Mapping Studies in Software Engineering (Petersen, Feldt, Mujtaba, Mattsson, 2008)

## Source structure and scope
Blekinge Institute of Technology / Ericsson authors; 10-page paper (EASE 2008, author PDF).
Defines a systematic mapping process, applies it to one map (software product line
variability, Mujtaba et al. 2008), and compares maps with ten SE systematic reviews.
Engagement depth: full text read. The process is described and applied once; there is no
controlled evaluation of whether the keywording procedure improves classification coverage.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** a systematic map "is a defined method to build a classification scheme and
  structure a software engineering field of interest"; analysis focuses on publication
  frequencies per category, "Thereby, the coverage of the research field can be determined."
- **§2, Fig. 1 process:** research questions (review scope) -> conduct search (all papers) ->
  screening (relevant papers) -> keywording using abstracts (classification scheme) -> data
  extraction and mapping (systematic map).
- **§2.2 Search:** search strings structured by population, intervention, comparison,
  outcome (after Kitchenham & Charters 2007). The authors deliberately did not restrict by
  outcome or study design: "If we had only considered certain types of studies the overview
  could have been biased and the map incomplete." Their variability map searched only the
  main forums (SPLC, PFE) plus journals.
- **§2.4 Keywording (Fig. 2):** keywording "is a way to reduce the time needed in developing
  the classification scheme and ensuring that the scheme takes the existing studies into
  account." Two steps: reviewers read abstracts for keywords/concepts reflecting the
  contribution and context; keywords from different papers are combined to form "a set of
  categories which is representative of the underlying population"; poor abstracts prompt
  reading introduction/conclusion; final keywords "can be clustered and used to form the
  categories". The prior map (Bailey et al. 2007) did not clearly describe how its scheme was
  created.
- **§2.4 facets:** three facets — topic facet derived from keywords (e.g. architecture,
  requirements, implementation variability), contribution-type facet (process, method, tool,
  etc.) derived from keywords, and a research-type facet adopted from an existing generic
  classification (Wieringa et al. 2006: validation research, evaluation research, solution
  proposal, philosophical, opinion, experience papers; Table 3).
- **§2.5:** "the classification scheme evolves while doing the data extraction, like adding
  new categories or merging and splitting existing categories"; reviewers record a short
  rationale per placement. Frequencies per category show "which categories have been
  emphasized in past research and thus to identify gaps". Bubble plots cross facets (Fig. 3).
- **§3.2 Classifying the topic area:** restricting to rigorous empirical papers (e.g. "only 5 %"
  rigorous in one review) risks an incomplete overview; empirical research may be easier in
  some sub-areas, so method-filtered samples bias the picture of the area.
- **§3.2 Validity:** one review found 73% of papers designated incorrectly (e.g. promised an
  experiment that was not one); maps that do not read deeply "might" make judgemental errors
  in detailed categories.
- **§4 guidelines:** use maps as a first step before a systematic review; "Adaptive Reading
  Depth" — abstracts "are often misleading and lack important information", so read more of a
  paper when classification is unclear; maps and reviews are "different points on a
  continuum".
