# Hypermedia Support for Argumentation-Based Rationale: 15 Years on from gIBIS and QOC

## Source structure and scope
Simon J. Buckingham Shum, Albert M. Selvin, Maarten Sierhuis, Jeffrey Conklin, Charles B. Haley, Bashar
Nuseibeh. Technical Report KMI-05-18 (Open University, December 2005); preprint of a chapter in
"Rationale Management in Software Engineering" (Dutoit, McCall, Mistrik, Paech eds., Springer 2006).
16 pages; sections 5.1–5.4 and 5.5.2, 5.8 read closely. Authors include co-creators of QOC and gIBIS —
insider retrospective.

## Anchored details
- **§5.1:** IBIS/QOC graphical argumentation maps are classed as "prescriptive, intrusive"; their
  intrusiveness is "an obstacle to adoption", leading many to conclude argument-map DR is "yet another
  failure of exciting research ideas to overcome the harsh realities of actual day-to-day practice". Since
  the 1996 DR book there was "subsequent decline in activity as the challenges of truly embedding
  argumentation-based DR in work practices sank in"; "it turned out to be the human factors that required
  closer attention".
- **§5.3 (capture problem):** "How does one acquire quality input to a rationale management system,
  without disrupting the very process it is designed to support". Grudin's point: no designer can be
  expected to enter rationale "solely for the possible benefit of a possibly unknown person at an unknown
  point in the future"; "There must be immediate value."
- **§5.3–5.4:** "A 1994 survey [Buckingham Shum & Hammond, IJHCS 40(4)] found comparatively weak evidence
  regarding usability and utility compared to what might have been expected given the scale of system
  development efforts."
- **§5.4 (cognitive overhead):** a study of software designers learning QOC on paper found four
  interleaving tasks: "unbundling" (separating ideas bundled together), "classification" (Question, Option
  or Criterion), "naming", and "structuring". Quoted: "deeper understanding of a domain comes through the
  discipline of expressing knowledge within a structural framework".
- **§5.5.2 (templates):** "A modeling approach focuses attention on a specific subset of issues and
  information, it may constrain the kinds of options one considers, and it may also focus attention on how
  one assesses them." Issue templates give beginners "a representational scaffold"; "Assuming the meeting
  has faith in the template, when its questions have been answered, the meeting can be confident that they
  have made some progress."
- **§5.8:** Compendium used on "over 100 projects"; empirical evidence of learnability/effectiveness is
  pointed to in field case analyses and video analyses (not reproduced in this chapter).
- References list: MacLean A, Young R, Bellotti V, Moran T (1991) "Questions, options and criteria:
  Elements of design space analysis"; MacLean, Bellotti, Shum (1993) "Developing the design space with
  design space analysis".
