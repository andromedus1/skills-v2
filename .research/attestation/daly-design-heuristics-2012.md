# Design Heuristics in Engineering Concept Generation (Daly, Yilmaz, Christian, Seifert & Gonzalez 2012)

## Source structure and scope
Shanna R. Daly, Seda Yilmaz, James L. Christian, Colleen M. Seifert, Richard Gonzalez; Journal of
Engineering Education 101(4), 601–629, October 2012. 29-page PDF read via text extraction: structured
abstract, introduction/background (fixation, ideation techniques, Table 1), method, findings (approaches
to idea generation), limitations and conclusions. Protocol study: think-aloud, sketches and retrospective
interviews from 36 engineering students and practitioners on one novel design task (solar cooking).

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** existing ideation strategies "are not based on evidence from designers or rigorous testing
  through empirical studies"; brainstorming "lacks specific ways to generate designs". Results: "evidence
  for over 60 strategies for concept generation". Design Heuristics defined as "cognitive prompts that
  facilitate exploration of multiple designs during concept generation".
- **Background (fixation paragraph):** "When an individual designer ... creates a potential solution, they often
  continue pursuing this single idea at the expense of exploring other alternatives" — "premature closure
  of the search process, defined as fixation"; "even years of professional experience were not enough to
  avoid fixation" (citing Jansson & Smith 1991; Purcell & Gero 1996, as reported).
- **Background (ideation techniques paragraph):** TRIZ "focuses on refinements of engineering mechanisms and design trade-offs
  that arise in the implementation phase"; "Only a few of these approaches have received any systematic
  empirical support." TRIZ derives from patents, but "a patent represents the outcome of hundreds of hours
  of design work rather than the initial stages of idea generation"; "no scientific evidence has been
  collected that documents the use of TRIZ principles by untrained engineers."
- **Background (team ideation paragraph, pp.605–606):** brainstorming groups produced fewer ideas than an equivalent number of
  individuals working alone (Lewis et al. 1975), "consistent among most studies focusing on Osborn's
  brainstorming" (as reported). For other Table 1 methods "little systematic evidence has been presented
  for their usefulness in engineering design."
- **Table 1:** morphological analysis = "Listing properties and functions of a design solution and
  multiple options for achieving each, then forming combinations to generate concepts" (Allen 1962;
  Zwicky 1969); SCAMPER, SIT, TRIZ described as transformations/modifications "to existing concepts".
- **Findings (p.612):** 36 engineers generated 179 concepts (max 9, min 1, mean 5 per participant). "No
  participants stated that their approach to concept generation was based on existing ideation methods ...
  in no cases did the participants follow the prescribed procedure of the method". A participant who varied
  one functional dimension was coded separately because it "did not capture the multidimensional aspect of
  morphological analysis".
- **Conclusions and Limitations sections:** Design Heuristics "help an individual build upon that idea to create
  endless variations, combining and developing ideas in unusual directions". Limitations: "only one design
  task"; "laboratory setting, with an artificial task and time frame".
