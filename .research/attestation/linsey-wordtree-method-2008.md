# WordTrees: A Method for Design-by-Analogy (Linsey, Wood & Markman)

## Source structure and scope
Julie Linsey (Texas A&M), Kristin Wood (UT Austin), Arthur Markman (UT Austin). ASEE 2008 Annual Conference,
paper AC 2008-1669, 14 pages. Read in full: abstract, §2 formal design-by-analogy methods, §3 method, §4
WordTree Lite, §5 case study, §6 conclusions.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** "Currently available design methods provide little guidance for systematically identifying
  potential analogous solutions"; the WordTree Method "begins with the functions and customer needs of the
  problem" and "re-represent[s] these functions and customer needs so that alternative retrieval cues can be
  developed", effective for retrieval "from their memory and from databases"; "A case study illustrates and
  evaluates the method."
- **§1:** "Over one hundred formal idea generation techniques have been developed ... Unfortunately, little
  empirical data exists to guide the use of these methods for engineering design."
- **§3 principle:** "design problems need to be represented in multiple ways ranging from very domain specific to
  general, thereby providing a variety of retrieval cues"; "effective retrieval cues depend on how the information
  was initially stored"; functional models are another likely-effective representation; TRIZ re-represents the
  problem "as the conflict between two generalized engineering parameters".
- **§3 steps:** key problem descriptors = single-word action verbs from functions and customer needs; build
  WordTrees by rotational brainwriting, with hypernyms (more general verbs) above and troponyms (more specific)
  below; augment with WordNet — unusual words "are frequently very domain specific verbs (functions) in distant
  but analogous domains"; identify and research potential analogies and analogous domains (patents, web search);
  write new problem statements from domain-specific to general; run idea generation with two teams, one of which
  "is unfamiliar with the problem" and sees only general and alternative-domain statements, because "individuals
  tend to focus too much on the surface and unimportant features of the problem rather than the causal
  structure" (authors' belief, stated as such).
- **§3:** cites prior work that "uncommon solution[s] ... tend to increase the number of ideas generated and not
  cause fixation".
- **§5–6 evidence:** single case study (device to fold laundry for students with severe disabilities); WordTree
  for "fold" surfaced sailing (douse/reef a sail) and bell-making ("stickle") domains; outcomes compared with a
  graduate team's design; "produced a significant number of analogies not evaluated by the original design team";
  "Much more evaluation of the WordTree Method needs to be completed".
