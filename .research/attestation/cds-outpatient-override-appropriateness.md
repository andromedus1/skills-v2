# Overrides of medication-related clinical decision support alerts in outpatients

## Source structure and scope
Karen C Nanji, Sarah P Slight, Diane L Seger, and co-authors (Massachusetts General Hospital, Partners Healthcare,
University of Durham, and others). J Am Med Inform Assoc. 2014;21(3):487-491, epub 2013 Oct 28, doi
10.1136/amiajnl-2013-001813 (as read on PMC). Retrospective analysis of three years (2009-2011) of outpatient CDS
alert logs at one academic center using an in-house electronic record, plus a chart review of 600 randomly sampled
overrides for appropriateness, judged against consensus criteria (inter-rater kappa 0.89).

## Anchored details
- **Abstract, Results:** "We reviewed 157 483 CDS alerts (7.9% alert rate) on 2 004 069 medication orders during the
  study period. 82 889 (52.6%) of alerts were overridden."

  > An average of 53% of overrides were classified as appropriate, and rates of appropriateness varied by alert type (p<0.0001) from 12% for renal recommendations to 92% for patient allergies.

- **Results, Table 1:** override rates by type ranged "from 24.4% to 85.0%": formulary substitution 85.0%, age-based
  79.0%, renal 78.0%, patient allergy 77.4%, drug-drug interaction 60.2%, duplicate drug 28.6%.
- **Results, Table 3 (appropriateness of overrides):** allergy 92%, drug-class interaction 88%, duplicate drug 82%,
  class-class 69%, formulary substitution 57%, age-based 39%, drug-drug interaction 12%, renal 12%; average 53%.
- **Discussion:** "Half of the overrides were appropriate, and the proportion that was appropriate varied even more by
  alert type." Alert types with mostly appropriate overrides "might be particularly good targets for refinement to
  reduce alert fatigue by reducing the number of alerts", while high inappropriate-override rates for drug-drug
  interactions "indicate the need for further education and intervention." The authors note that alert committees
  "add alerts incrementally, and unless an aggressive approach is taken to eliminate unnecessary alerts, more are
  generally included over time." They cite a tiering study in which "For the most severe alerts, none were overridden
  at the tiered site, versus a 66% override rate at the non-tiered site". A prior study at the same center (2006)
  found "33% of interruptive CDS alerts in outpatient systems were overridden".
- **Limitations:** single center and single in-house CDS system; appropriateness on a sample; "we did not
  specifically assess whether patients were harmed as a result of alert overrides."
