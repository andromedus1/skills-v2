# Scaling up analogical innovation with crowds and AI (Kittur, Yu, Hope, Chan, Lifshitz-Assaf, Gilon, Ng, Kraut & Shahaf)

## Source structure and scope
Aniket Kittur and coauthors. PNAS 116(6) 1870–1877 (2019), PMC6369801. Read the full PMC HTML text: abstract,
introduction, Fixation, Scalability, Complexity, summary and challenges sections. It is a perspective: study
results are the authors' summaries of their own separate papers, mostly without sample sizes in this text.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** moving analogy "out of a single person's mind and distributing it across many information processors,
  both human and machine ... has the potential to overcome cognitive fixation, scale to large idea repositories, and
  support complex problems with multiple constraints"; reports "first steps ... that quantitatively demonstrate the
  promise of the approach, as well as core challenges".
- **Introduction:** "the sensitivity of human memory to surface similarity means that people often become fixated on
  surface-level details that prevent them from retrieving distant analogs or applying them"; "scalability, fixation,
  and complexity" named as the three barriers.
- **Fixation section:** "Human retrieval is highly sensitive to surface similarities, favoring 'near,' or within-domain,
  analogs ... over 'far,' or structurally similar analogs from different domains"; prior approaches help designers
  "generate multiple abstract representations of their problem" or use "functional ontologies"; the paper's approach
  "separates people who develop schemas of an original problem from people who find inspirations and solve the
  problem, thus avoiding fixation".
- **Schema pipeline (summary of Yu et al.):** abstraction → inspiration search on schemas → solve the concrete problem
  "led to significantly more novel and useful ideas than traditional design techniques, such as brainstorming or using
  concrete examples"; with an abstract schema "people explored significantly more diverse domains"; staging "allowed
  intermediate evaluations". Problem-based ideation: crowds given the abstract schema "found significantly more analogs
  that matched the deep problem structure"; ideas from schema-inspired analogs were more creative.
- **Scalability — two-stage domain search:** "without guidance, people select domains to search based on surface
  similarity to the problem's domain"; crowds first named "domains of expertise that are distant from the initial
  problem but relevant enough", e.g. prompted to "suggest three types of experts who might provide useful or
  interesting perspectives in solving [the schematic problem]"; for "fit objects of different sizes into a
  container" workers named contortionists, carpenters, Japanese-aesthetics experts vs computer technicians and
  electricians from the original description; adapting analogs from the abstract condition "generated significantly
  more creative ideas".
- **AI retrieval:** surface-statistics methods (embeddings, LSI, LDA) "excel at detecting surface similarity" but "are
  often unable to detect deep relational similarity", especially across domains; purpose–mechanism inspirations led to
  "approximately twice as many ideas rated as good by judges blind to condition"; for research papers,
  purpose–mechanism vectors found known analogies with "substantially higher precision", and in one expert case study
  gave "twice as many papers that the expert identified as valuable, unexplored sources".
- **Complexity:** decomposing a kindergarten-chair problem into constraints; "schemas that made the constraint concrete
  (e.g., prevent tipping over) led to more relevant inspirations that were still distant from the original problem
  domain" than higher-level schemas (e.g., safety), and "significantly better chair designs".
- **Challenges:** "We have explored only a small number of the possible configurations ... (including several
  unsuccessful ones not discussed here)"; humans needed for "vetting candidate inspirations retrieved by AI, applying
  them ..., and integrating across multiple potentially conflicting inspirations"; domain expertise has positive
  effects but also "greater fixation on defining the problem, finding solutions, and evaluating their suitability";
  "our research has not yet tackled the role of human expertise."
