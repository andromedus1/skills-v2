# Cross-study Reliability of the Open Card Sorting Method

Christos Katsanos, Nikolaos Tselios, Nikolaos Avouris, Stavros Demetriadis, Ioannis
Stamelos, Lefteris Angelis. CHI 2019 Extended Abstracts (Late-Breaking Work LBW2718),
DOI 10.1145/3290607.3312999 (as printed). Full text read (6 pp.).

## Source structure and scope
Six open card sorts, 140 tech-savvy participants (students/practitioners): three sorts of
38 travel-website items (n = 34, 26, 22) and three of 55 e-shop items (n = 22, 16, 20).
Questions: do different studies with similar-profile participants yield consistent
groupings, clusters and navigation schemes?

## Anchored details
- **Introduction:** "Despite the popularity of the card sorting method, its cross-study
  reliability has not been examined."
- **Methodology:** cites research suggesting "a minimum of 15 users to obtain robust data
  from open card sorts".
- **RQ1 (Tables 2–5):** distance-matrix Spearman correlations 0.419–0.851; Mantel tests
  (10,000 permutations) 0.609–0.930, all significant (p < 0.001). Lowest agreement:
  Travel3 vs Travel1/Travel2 (Spearman 0.419/0.432; Mantel 0.609/0.633).
- **RQ2 (Table 6):** base-cluster similarity 90.9%–96.5% across studies.
- **RQ3 (Table 7):** "where to cut the line in the dendrogram … greatly affects the final
  navigation scheme. Given the same dendrogram, two people can produce different content
  structures." Elbow-criterion navigation schemes agreed 63.2%–92.7% (percentage of items
  grouped together in both structures).
- **Limitations:** ignores qualitative data (group labels, comments); other factors for
  final scheme not considered; needs more domains and card counts.
- **Conclusion:** findings "tend to provide support for the cross-study reliability of card
  sorting", justifying running one study; multiple-mental-model concern "may not be that
  strong, at least for similar profile participants." Test–retest reliability left to
  future work.
