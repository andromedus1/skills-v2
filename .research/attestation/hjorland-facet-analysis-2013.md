# Facet analysis: the logical approach to knowledge organization

Birger Hjørland. ISKO Encyclopedia of Knowledge Organization entry (version 1.0
published 2018-10-25, last updated 2020-12-15), an HTML version of the preprint of
Hjørland 2013, Information Processing and Management 49(2): 545–557 (per the page
colophon). Full entry read.

## Source structure and scope
§1 Introduction; §2 Historical developments (Sayers, Ranganathan, British Classification
Research Group); §3 Basic principles; §4 Construction principles for facet
classification schemes; §5 Relations to Aristotelian logic; §6 Reception and criticism;
§7 Discussion; endnotes.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** facet analysis (FA) is "mostly based on principles of logical division
  developed more than two millennia ago"; strength = logical principles and structure;
  "main weaknesses are (1) its lack of empirical basis and (2) its speculative ordering of
  knowledge"; relies on "the problematic assumption that relations between concepts are a
  priori".
- **§2, Vickery (1960, 12) quoted:** "The essence of facet analysis is the sorting of terms
  in a given field of knowledge into homogeneous, mutually exclusive facets, each derived
  from single characteristic of division."
- **§3:** Ranganathan's PMEST fundamental categories (Personality, Matter, Energy, Space,
  Time), which Ranganathan held "necessary and sufficient" for all documents; Vickery's
  longer list of 13 categories (substance, organ, constituent, structure, shape, property,
  object of action, action, operation, process, agent, space, time). Analytico-synthetic
  method: analysis breaks a subject into basic concepts; synthesis combines them —
  "classes are constructed when needed by combinations of building blocks" vs enumerative
  schemes that list all classes. Hjørland's critique: it "does not follow that it is
  possible for FC to represent all new subjects as combinations of existing elements"
  (Kuhnian incommensurability: new conceptual structures need new concepts). Vickery's
  expansion "may imply that there is not a fixed set of categories"; "still unclear
  whether categories are discovered or constructed … and how their identification can be
  verified by other scholars."
- **§4 construction steps (after La Barre 2010, modified):** (1) define subject field —
  what entities and what aspects interest the user group (Vickery 1966); (2) facet
  formulation from "a representative range of material" into homogeneous groups;
  (3) facet amplification and structuring — building hierarchies "helps to coalesce
  synonyms, eliminate terms that are collated with the wrong facet, and to indicate gaps
  in the system"; (4) scope notes; (5) facet arrangement (citation order, use-dependent);
  (6) notation. Principles of logical division: "Only one characteristic of division should
  be applied at a time"; "Division should not make a leap"; "Division should be
  exhaustive." Citation-order choice admits "some degree of arbitrariness"; "There is no
  suggestion on how empirically to determine what citation order is 'helpful'".
- **§4, Mills (2004) on Farradane:** an inductive approach yielded classifications
  "remarkably similar" to faceted ones; endnote 11: Hjørland "not been able to identify any
  empirical evidence" for that claim; endnote 10: assumes inductive principles "produce
  different classifications compared to those classified by deductive (rationalist)
  methods".
- **§5, Parry & Hacker (1991) rules quoted:** coordinate classes mutually exclusive;
  jointly coextensive with the superordinate class; one principle of classification per
  stage; fewest principles preferred; informal: serve the purpose; be as fruitful as
  possible. Also: "In the social and natural sciences, a priori divisions are generally of
  relatively limited value." J.S. Mill's natural vs artificial classification: natural
  classification groups objects about which the most and most important general
  propositions can be made; the Linnaean stamen count is an artificial example.
- **§6 Reception:** "It is rare in the literature of FC to find discussions of the
  limitations of its approach or comparisons with other approaches." Cranfield I results
  (after Vickery 1966): Facet 73.8%, UDC 75.6%, Alphabetical 81.5%, Uniterm 82.0%
  (supplementary Facet 83%). Miksa (1998, 73): Ranganathan's faceted structure "may well
  have represented his need to find more order and regularity, in the realm of subjects,
  than actually exist." Endnote 7: categories may "force a discipline into a particular
  form, while being blind to other categories and forms".
- **§7 Discussion:** ordering empirical material in logical categories makes it "much
  easier … to evaluate the term base, for example by finding missing terms and aspects";
  conclusion that FA "cannot stand alone: rationalist methods have to be supplemented with
  empirical, historicist and pragmatic approaches"; "it has never been outlined how to
  design and evaluate classification for different purposes"; calls for "comparative
  studies of classifications designed from the alternative methodologies".
