# General Morphological Analysis: A general method for non-quantified modelling (Ritchey)

## Source structure and scope
Tom Ritchey, Swedish Morphological Society; adapted from "Fritz Zwicky, Morphologie and Policy
Analysis" (16th EURO Conference, Brussels, 1998), copyright 2002–2013. Ten-page PDF read in full.
Sections: Introduction; Methodological background; General morphology; Cross-consistency assessment;
Conclusions; References. Practitioner/advocate text by the method's principal modern developer; it
reports applications, not controlled comparisons.

## Anchored details
- **Introduction (p.1):** GMA, developed by Fritz Zwicky (Caltech), is "a method for structuring and
  investigating the total set of relationships contained in multi-dimensional, non-quantifiable, problem
  complexes"; computer support developed at the Swedish Defence Research Agency in 1995; "more than 100
  projects" since then.
- **General morphology (p.3):** construction — "identifying and defining the parameters (or dimensions)
  of the problem complex ... and assigning each parameter a range of relevant 'values' or conditions";
  the "Zwicky box" is the n-dimensional matrix; each cell (one value from each parameter) "marks out a
  particular state or configuration". Worked example 5x5x3 = 75 cells. The morphological field format
  (parameters as columns) removes the three-dimension limit of typological tables.
- **p.4:** "One of the advantages of GMA is that there are no formal constraints to mixing and comparing
  such different types of issues" (technical, financial, political, ethical).
- **p.5:** Shelter-program field segment: 6 parameters, 4x4x4x3x3x4 = 2304 configurations; full original
  10-parameter field ">500,000 possible configurations – far too many to deal with by hand". "It is
  fairly easy – by hand – to identify and mark out a few dozen realistic policy configurations."
- **Cross-consistency assessment (pp.5–6):** reduce the formally possible configurations to internally
  consistent ones ("solution space"); Zwicky's "principle of contradiction and reduction". All parameter
  values are compared pair-wise "in the manner of a cross-impact matrix"; judgement is about mutual
  consistency, "no reference here to causality or direction". If a pair is incompatible, all
  configurations containing it are inconsistent.
- **p.6 — three inconsistency types:** "purely logical contradictions", "empirical inconsistencies
  (i.e. relationships judged to be highly improbable or implausible on empirical grounds)", and
  "normative constraints". Normative judgements are deferred: "We must first investigate what is
  possible, before making judgements about what is, and what is not, desirable."
- **p.6 — scaling claim:** configurations grow exponentially with parameters, pair-wise relations only
  quadratically; "a morphological field involving as many as 100,000 formal configurations requires no
  more than a few hundred pair-wise evaluations".
- **Conclusions (p.8):** Zwicky called the approach "totality research" which, in an "unbiased way
  attempts to derive all the solutions of any given problem"; it "may help us to discover new
  relationships or configurations ... which we might have overlooked by other – less systematic –
  methods"; encourages investigating "boundary conditions". "Poorly defined parameters become
  immediately (and embarrassingly) evident when they are cross-referenced" — "a form of garbage
  detection". Audit trail claim: judgments "relatively traceable, and – in a certain sense – even
  reproducible".
- **p.8 — divergence trials:** "We have run trials in which identical morphological fields were
  presented to different groups for cross-consistency assessment. Comparing the results, and bringing the
  groups together to discuss diverging assessments ... tells us something about the effects of group
  composition on the assessments." No quantitative results of these trials are reported.
