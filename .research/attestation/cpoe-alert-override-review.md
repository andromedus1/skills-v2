# Overriding of Drug Safety Alerts in Computerized Physician Order Entry

## Source structure and scope
Heleen van der Sijs, Jos Aarts, Arnold Vulto, Marc Berg (Erasmus University Medical Center, Rotterdam). J Am Med
Inform Assoc. 2006 Mar-Apr;13(2):138-147, doi 10.1197/jamia.M1809, PMCID PMC1447540 (as read on PMC). A literature
review of 17 publications on physicians' overriding of unsolicited drug safety alerts in computerized order entry,
nine with quantitative override data, interpreted through Reason's accident-causation model. The authors note that
alert levels "cannot be compared between studies because standardization of alert levels is absent."

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:**

  > Drug safety alerts are overridden by clinicians in 49% to 96% of cases. Alert overriding may often be justified and adverse drug events due to overridden alerts are not always preventable.

  "A distinction between appropriate and useful alerts should be made."
- **Results, "How Often and in What Kinds of Situations Are Safety Alerts Overridden?" / Table 2:** "Except for
  serious alerts for overdose, which are overridden in one fourth of all alerts, safety alerts are overridden in 49%
  to 96% of cases." "Bates et al. propose a maximum override rate of 40% but do not offer an explanation for this
  figure." One study "showed an increase in override rates from about 50% to 75% during a five-year period". Low-level
  alerts "appear to be overridden more often than high-level alerts", not fully confirmed. "A relationship between
  relative amount of alerts and percentages overridden cannot be observed, but this may be due to the small number of
  studies."
- **"Factors That Play a Role in Overriding":** "The most important reason for overriding was alert fatigue caused
  by poor signal-to-noise ratio because the alert was not serious, was irrelevant, or was shown repeatedly." Alert
  fatigue is described as the state "which can cause important alerts to be ignored along with clinically unimportant
  ones." "Twenty-two percent of general practitioners admitted drug interaction overriding without checking."
  Two studies found "36.5% and 39% of the alerts were false positive"; "Reviewers agreed with clinicians' decisions in
  95.6% of cases where physicians overrode a valid alert"; "48% of the true positive alerts were overridden" in one
  study.
- **"Effect of Overridden Alerts":** adverse events in "2.3%, 2.5%, and 6% of the overridden alerts"; a high override
  rate "might also result in the hospital decision to turn off a whole group of alerts, including relevant alerts".
  "Monitoring of overriding is said to be necessary to keep the override rate within acceptable limits".
- **Figure 3 caption (definitions):** "Appropriate alerts = true positive alerts; overridden alerts = alerts that did
  not result in cancellation or change of order".
- **"Understanding the Effect...":** "only unjustified overriding (ignoring alerts, misinterpretation, wrong
  selection) poses a problem." Requiring reasons for overriding "triggers physicians to rethink" and "can help adjust
  the knowledge database."
- **"Improving Alerting" / Table 3:** suggestions include alerts being clinically relevant, presenting justification
  ("no black-box warnings"), clear seriousness, directing low-specificity alerts to pharmacists or nurses, preventing
  "Annoying repetition", and "Overriding fatal alerts should not be easy (high threshold)". "If physicians ignore
  alerts, they often do so because alerts are inappropriate." The first step proposed is "reducing the number of
  inappropriate (false positive) alerts". "Alerts with lower levels of seriousness can be absent or shown
  nonintrusively." "Turning off alerts without careful error management may impair patient safety."
