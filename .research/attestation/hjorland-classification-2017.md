# Classification

Birger Hjørland. ISKO Encyclopedia of Knowledge Organization entry, version 1.0
published 2017-02-09, current version 1.21 (2025-01-14); version 1.0 also published as
Knowledge Organization 44(2): 97–128 (per colophon). Read §4 "Theories of
classification" in full and the endnotes; §§1–3 (word meaning, related terms) skimmed.

## Anchored details
- **§4.1 classical vs prototype views:** Aristotelian (monothetic) classes are defined by
  necessary and sufficient characteristics; Wittgenstein's family resemblance and Rosch's
  prototype theory treat classes as graded. Lakoff (1987, 6) quoted: the classical theory
  "was not the result of empirical study … It was a philosophical position arrived at on
  the basis of a priori speculation." Bowker (1998, 256) quoted: one can "name a population
  of objects that people would in general agree to call chairs that have no two binary
  features in common". Summary lesson: "Instead of the classical model of sets of mutually
  exclusive and jointly exhaustive criteria, we may need alternative models."
- **Endnote 5:** monothetic class = characteristics necessary and sufficient; polythetic
  class = "a broad set of criteria that are neither necessary nor sufficient. Each member
  of the category must possess a certain minimal number of defining characteristics, but
  none of the features has to be found in each member".
- **§4.2b Marradi (1990):** intensional classification (subdivision, downwards, one
  fundamentum divisionis per step) vs extensional classification (grouping by perceived
  similarity; numerical taxonomy, cluster analysis), "The predominant criterion is to
  maximize homogeneity within classes and heterogeneity between classes"; plus "classing"
  (assigning objects to existing classes).
- **§4.2c four approaches:** rationalism (logical division; criticised for essentialism —
  the basis of division must be "deduced from what are considered essential criteria");
  empiricism (phenetics) — "If we assume that any object has an unlimited number of
  properties, then it is impossible to consider them all … By implication, any empirical
  classification is biased in ways that cannot be controlled"; "Raw data is an oxymoron";
  "things cannot be similar in an objective way … For any three objects, two different
  classifications can be constructed" (colour vs shape figure); birds/reptiles grouped
  differently by numerical taxonomy and by phylogenetic methods; historicism (genealogical
  /cladistic classification by common ancestry); pragmatism/critical theory — "a
  classification can never be neutral"; Nobes & Stadler (2013) quoted: "objectivity is
  neither possible nor desirable in classification. Despite the arbitrariness, some
  classifications can be more reasonable or more useful than others"; Shera's tree example
  (organism, aesthetic entity, income source) for purpose-relative classes.
- **Dupré quoted (§4.2c):** "there is very likely no such ideal classification. There is no
  reason why a classification that reflects the origins of the things classified should
  coincide exactly with one aimed at the ecological relations of those things".
- **Endnote 9 (Mayr 1995 quoted):** reliance on single characters and "the inevitable
  arbitrariness of the sequence in which these characters were chosen — could lead to
  rather artificial systems"; "the downward procedure actually produces identification
  schemes rather than classifications".
- **Endnote 10 (Mayr quoting Whewell 1840):** "The maxim by which all systems professing to
  be natural must be tested is this: that the arrangement obtained from one set of
  characters coincides with the arrangement obtained from another set".
- **Endnote 12 (Medin & Aguilar 1999 quoted):** similarity is "too unconstrained to be
  useful as an explanatory principle"; following Goodman, "any two things share an
  unlimited number of properties".
- **Endnote 16 (Marradi 1990 quoted):** Cohen and Nagel — any division by an arbitrarily
  chosen trait is natural yet artificial "in the sense that we select the trait";
  Tiryakian: "the reification of typologies is a frequent temptation and pitfall".
- **§4.3:** taxonomic monism vs pluralism; is there one correct classification.
