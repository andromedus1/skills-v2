# On the Benefits and Pitfalls of Analogies for Innovative Design: Ideation Performance Based on Analogical Distance, Commonness, and Modality of Examples (Chan, Fu, Schunn, Cagan, Wood & Kotovsky)

## Source structure and scope
Joel Chan, Katherine Fu, Christian Schunn, Jonathan Cagan, Kristin Wood, Kenneth Kotovsky. JMD 133,
081004, August 2011 (publisher PDF hosted by a university lab site). Read: abstract, §3 method, §4 metrics,
§5.2 results by distance and commonness.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** drawing on examples by analogy "can be a powerful tool ... but also carries the risk of negative
  design outcomes (e.g., design fixation)"; senior engineering students generated concepts with or without
  US-patent examples crossed by "analogical distance (near-field vs. far-field), commonness (more vs.
  less-common), and modality (picture vs. text)", plus a no-example control; "positive effects of far-field and
  less-common examples on novelty and variability in quality"; "not modulated by modality"; "the combination of
  far-field, less-common examples resulted in more novel concepts than in the control group."
- **§3.2:** 153 students randomly assigned across nine conditions; examples given after an initial ideation
  phase.
- **§5.2.1 distance:** far-field examples increased solution transfer from examples (d = 0.60) — near-field
  example features "were no more likely to be present" than pre-example; far-field reduced quantity relative to
  near-field (d = −0.30); "no reliable effects of distance on breadth of search (p = 0.78)"; no effect on mean
  or maximum quality; far-field increased variability in quality vs near (d = 0.64) and vs none (d = 0.78);
  far-field raised mean novelty (d = 0.56) and maximum novelty (d = 0.56) vs near-field, but "no significant
  differences between ... no examples (control) vs. near- or far-field examples on either mean or maximum
  novelty." Cost: "about one solution concept" fewer in standard time.
- **§5.2.2 commonness:** more-common examples reduced quantity (vs less-common d = −0.67; vs none d = −0.76)
  and reduced breadth of search — "participants who received more-common examples searched less of the design
  space" than less-common (d = −0.61) or no examples (d = −1.03); less-common examples increased variability in
  quality.
