# Developing Theoretically Informed Typologies in International Business: Why We Need Them, and How to Do It

Matthew M. C. Allen, Geoff Wood, Mehmet Demirbag. Accepted version (Essex repository),
marked forthcoming in Journal of International Business Studies. Full text read
(33 pp. accepted manuscript).

## Source structure and scope
Argument for Weberian explanatory typologies and ideal types; contrast of classification
systems vs typologies; five-step process (conceptual clarity; justifying dimensions and
role of theory; modelling and operationalization; "confronting" the typology with
evidence; post-empirics theory re-assessment); two cautions.

## Anchored details
- **Classification vs typology (pp. 6–7):** "Simple classification systems are a means to
  order phenomena into mutually exclusive and exhaustive categories, often drawing on a
  series of discrete decision rules and empirical evidence" (citing Bailey 1994; Doty &
  Glick 1994); explanatory typologies are sets of inter-related ideal types relying on
  socially constructed concepts; "different ideal types may be the same or similar on at
  least some of their dimensions, but may also vary in respect to others." Second
  difference: "theory or logic underpins the types within the former, but not the
  categories or classes in the latter".
- **Ideal types (pp. 7–8):** not meant to capture real-world examples accurately; quotes
  Weber that the more abstract the ideal type, the better it performs in "formulating
  terminology, classifications, and hypotheses". Ideal types "should be theoretically
  consistent, probable/feasible in objective terms, and not contradicted by any existing
  knowledge". Constructed ideal types (Becker, McKinney) are more empirically informed.
- **Taxonomy vs typology (pp. 8–9):** "taxonomies are derived from empirical evidence, and
  typologies are largely conceptual" (citing Bailey 1994; Pugh et al. 1969); inductive
  configurational analyses yield taxonomies, deductive ones typologies.
- **Step One (p. 9):** identifying key concepts has "no specific method other than a
  mixture of prior knowledge and guidance from theory (Bailey, 1994)".
- **Step Two (p. 13):** researchers "should justify the relative importance of the chosen
  dimensions" and give a theoretical explanation for each ideal type's link to the
  outcome; the theory chosen informs the choice of dimensions.
- **State-capitalism example (p. 12):** dimensions of "purpose" "may not, theoretically or
  logically, be mutually exclusive", so regimes pursuing combinations form distinct types.
- **Step Three (pp. 15–16):** do not "assign actual individuals, companies or countries to
  an ideal type as if it were a category"; treating types as categories treats
  within-type differences as inconsequential (citing Doty et al. 1993 on Miles & Snow).
  Dimensions are conceptual abstractions; level of abstraction should match theory and
  evidence.
- **Step Five (pp. 18–19):** fuzzy-set QCA "may highlight how theoretically neglected
  configurations of causal conditions may lead to the outcome" (Witt & Jackson 2016
  example); Weber "did not offer detailed guidance on how the ideal types and typology may
  need to change".
- **Cautions (pp. 21–22):** typologies can become "static, discrete ideal types"; avoid
  reification — "treating ideal types as 'things'".
- **JIBS count (p. 5):** typology papers in JIBS titles/abstracts: 11 (1990s), 9 (2000s),
  7 (2010s).
