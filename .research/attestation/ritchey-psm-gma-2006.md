# Problem structuring using computer-aided morphological analysis (Ritchey 2006, JORS)

## Source structure and scope
Tom Ritchey; Journal of the Operational Research Society (2006) 57, 792–801 (post-peer-review,
pre-copyedit version, 12 pages, read in full). Sections: Introduction; Morphologies and typologies;
General morphology; Problem structuring with GMA; Structuring complex social planning problems with GMA
(Extended Producer Responsibility case); Conclusions. Advocate/practitioner account with one case study;
no comparative evaluation.

## Anchored details
- **Abstract:** GMA "relies on a constructed parameter space, linked by way of logical relationships,
  rather than on causal relationships and a hierarchal structure".
- **Introduction (p.2):** contrasts PSMs built on causal/influence networks (SSM, SODA) with
  morphological/typological methods built on "constructed parameter spaces"; rudimentary forms in the
  Strategic Choice Approach and QFD.
- **General morphology (pp.3–4):** Zwicky's 1947 "propulsive system morphology" — 6 parameters, 576
  formal configurations (Figure 1). Norris (1963) proposed morphology as a computer-supported engineering
  design method "to systematically separate and collate different design solutions" (as attributed by
  Ritchey).
- **p.4:** "GMA has been written about and discussed far more than it has actually been used in 'real'
  client-based projects", attributed by the author to manual execution; by hand, "working with as few as
  six or seven variables becomes a considerable task", so GMA "has usually been carried out as a
  relatively simple form of attribute listing with internal consistency checks" (p.5).
- **Problem structuring with GMA (p.5):** facilitated group process; "subject specialist groups of no
  more than 6-7 persons"; wider knowledge via specialists or "parallel groups"; 2–15 workshop days; two
  facilitators per group. "A typical morphological field of 6-10 variables can contain between 50,000
  and 5,000,000 formal configurations, far too many to inspect by hand."
- **p.6:** "a typical morphological field can be reduced by up to 90 or even 99%, depending on the
  problem structure. (Scenario fields are an exception...)". Two principal inconsistency types (logical,
  empirical); normative constraints "must be used with great care, and clearly designated as such".
  Reduced field becomes an "inference model" where any parameter can be input or output.
- **p.7:** "GMA requires strong, experienced facilitation, an engaged group of subject specialists and a
  good deal of patience."
- **EPR case (pp.7–9):** scenario field of 8 parameters = 20,736 configurations; scenario fields "are
  often difficult to assess internally and reduce" because "it is risky to exclude relationships which
  may seem improbable today"; instead select drivers, choose configurations, then check consistency.
  "eight specific configurations were chosen. Together, these covered all of the parameter states in the
  scenario field ('full field coverage'), and represented a broad range of futures projections."
  Strategy field 8 parameters = 34,560 configurations; CCA "reduced this to 480 strategies which were
  deemed realistic". Linking the two fields fully would give "over 700 million formal configurations";
  a condensed "scenario placeholder" is used instead.
- **p.9:** "it is not the morphological model as an end-product which is the sole important result ...
  Much of the utility ... is the process itself" (shared terminology).
- **Conclusions (p.11):** "the output of a morphological analysis is no better than the quality of its
  input"; claimed in-built "garbage detection" because "poorly defined parameters and incomplete ranges
  of conditions are immediately revealed when one begins the task of cross-consistency assessment".
